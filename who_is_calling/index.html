Front matter · Why read this book

Rule 4711

What an old firewall rule says about trusting addresses

On an ordinary Tuesday, an engineer on Granvik’s platform team was asked a simple question by an auditor: who can reach the payroll database?

The honest answer took two days to find. The database sat behind a firewall with about 4,200 rules. One of them, rule 4711, allowed TCP from 10.42.7.19 to the database on port 5432. It had been added in 2019 for a reporting server. That server had been retired years ago. The address had gone back into a pool, and since the move to containers it was handed out to pods. On the day of the audit it belonged to a test pod run by a team that had never heard of the payroll system. Nothing had been attacked. Nothing had broken. The rule had simply gone on saying “trust whatever is at this address” long after anybody knew what was there.

Granvik does not exist. It is a made-up Swedish public agency with about 3,000 staff, two data halls, a growing OpenShift estate, a long tail of virtual machines and physical servers, staff who log in through Entra ID, and a few systems in Azure. Everything about it is borrowed from organisations that do exist, and the story of rule 4711 is a composite of things that happen in all of them. This book follows Granvik’s platform team as they design an internal cloud where a rule like 4711 cannot be written, because no rule may mention an address at all.

What address-based trust assumes

For decades, networks decided whom to trust by where traffic came from. That habit rests on a handful of assumptions. Each one was reasonable once. None of them holds inside a modern platform.

What the old model assumes What actually happens Where this book deals with it
Inside the wall is safe. Attackers get in through phishing, a vulnerable app or a stolen VPN credential, and then inherit everything the inside is trusted with. Chapter 1
An address tells you who is talking. Nobody checks the source address. It is reused, shared behind NAT and changes whenever something moves. Chapter 2
A name tells you whom you reached. Plain DNS is unauthenticated. Only a certificate checked on connect proves who answered. Chapters 2 and 4
Encryption means the connection is safe. Encryption without a name check is “encrypted trust-everyone”. Authentication is not authorization. Chapters 5 and 8
A secret in a vault is a safe start. The workload needs a credential to fetch the secret. Someone has to hand out the first one, and that is where attacks aim. Chapter 6
The service knows who the user is. Behind a proxy or a service acting for a user, the downstream API sees only the middleman unless tokens carry both. Chapter 7
Firewall rules document our dependencies. Address rules drift from reality within weeks. Nobody can say why rule 4711 exists. Chapters 8 and 11
Namespaces and VLANs are boundaries. Kubernetes namespaces are not network boundaries by default, and a VLAN is a perimeter with a smaller wall. Chapter 9
One platform, one identity system. Pods, VMs, bare metal and cloud each have their own idea of identity, and calls cross between them all day. Chapter 10

The replacement is usually called zero trust: decide every request on who is asking, what they are asking for and whether explicit policy allows it, never on where the request came from. The phrase is used so loosely that it has almost stopped meaning anything. This book takes it apart into concrete mechanisms (keys, certificates, TLS, workload identity, tokens, policy engines, meshes and overlays) and shows what each one proves and what it does not.

Note · A useful way to think about it

Think of a large office building. The old model is a guard at the front door: once you are past the door you can walk into any room. The new model gives every person a badge that cannot be copied, puts a reader on every door, and keeps a list of which badges open which doors. The list is written in names and roles, not in “whoever is standing on the third floor”. The rest of the book is about making the badges, the readers and the list, and about what happens when one of them fails.

Why it matters to you

You have used most of these things already: certificates, TLS, tokens, Kubernetes, firewalls. But a sketchy picture of the foundations makes every later idea harder than it needs to be, and design reviews in this area are full of confident statements that do not survive a careful reading of the primary sources. “We use mTLS, so we have zero trust.” “The token is signed, so it is safe.” “Federation lets them in.” Each of those sentences is wrong in a specific way that this book explains.

This book will give you:

  • a vocabulary precise enough to read NIST SP 800-207, the SPIFFE specifications, the Istio ambient documentation and the OAuth RFCs, and understand what they are saying;
  • a mental model of what each mechanism proves, so that you can tell authentication from authorization, validation from permission, and a connection’s identity from a request’s;
  • the judgement to review a design and find the place where trust quietly widens: a missing audience check, a shared edge, a copied key, a rule that inherits more than it should;
  • a worked design, Granvik’s bubble model, that shows the ideas fitting together, including where they strain.

Who this book is for, how it is built, and how to read it

This book exists for one reason: so that you can open NIST SP 800-207, the SPIFFE specifications, Istio’s ambient documentation or an OAuth RFC, understand what they are saying, and then judge whether a design for an internal cloud holds up.

Who it is for, and what it assumes

It is written for engineers who design, build or review platforms: platform and infrastructure engineers, architects, security engineers and developers who find themselves in a room deciding how services should trust each other. It assumes you know nothing about the subject. You have probably used most of these things, but the book explains each one from the beginning anyway, because a sketchy picture of the foundations makes every later idea harder.

You do not need any mathematics. The cryptography chapter tells you what each tool proves and what it does not, not how it works inside.

How it is built

The eleven chapters climb in a fixed order, in four parts.

  • Part I, Why identity and not location (chapters 1 and 2), explains why network location stopped being a good reason to trust anything, and how networks actually move bytes.
  • Part II, The foundations (chapters 3 to 5), covers the cryptography, certificates and TLS that everything else stands on.
  • Part III, Identity and permission (chapters 6 to 8), covers the two kinds of identity, workloads and people, how one can act for the other, and how to decide what an identity may do.

The running example: Granvik and the bubble model

Granvik is a made-up Swedish public agency, introduced in the previous section. Its platform team wants an internal cloud where trust never depends on an address. Their design sketch is called the bubble model, and every chapter ends with a section, In the bubble model, that maps the chapter’s ideas onto it.

The sketch has a few rules:

  • Bubbles are nestable scopes that are the unit of ownership and trust. A bubble might be a team (gv/team-obs), a system inside it (gv/team-obs/loki) or a smaller part.
  • Atoms are the running things inside a bubble: a pod, a VM, a process. Identities are issued at this smallest level.
  • Trust is granted at whatever level is true. “Anything in team-app may push logs” is a grant to a whole bubble; “only the orders service may” is a grant to a smaller one. Trust inheritance stops at the lowest level where it is declared.
  • Bindings between bubbles are explicit and non-transitive: if X may call Y and Y may call Z, that says nothing about X and Z.
  • Names, not addresses. Bubbles and atoms have stable human names plus synthetic IDs. No rule anywhere contains an IP address.
  • Each bubble has a gateway at its edge, its “NAT”, and everything is declared in a GitOps repository, so the same repository can “bubble up” in another data hall.

The In the bubble model sections are honest about where an idea strains the model, not just where it fits. Chapter 11 assembles the whole design.

Granvik’s design sketch for an internal cloud: nestable bubbles that own atoms and services, identities issued per atom, trust granted at the level where it is true, explicit non-transitive bindings between bubbles, and stable names instead of addresses in every rule.

Chapter 01

Why Location Stopped Meaning Trust

From “where are you?” to “who are you, and may you do this?”

For decades, networks decided whom to trust by where traffic came from: inside the wall was safe, outside was not. That rule broke once attackers learned to get inside, and once the “inside” stopped being one place. Zero trust replaces “where are you?” with “who are you, what are you asking for, and should this specific request be allowed right now?”, decided per request, by explicit policy.

Before you start

The front matter told the story of rule 4711. This chapter names the model that produced it, the perimeter model, and introduces the vocabulary of its replacement.

The chapter in brief: the perimeter model; where it breaks; the zero trust idea; BeyondCorp; NIST SP 800-207’s vocabulary and tenets; where enforcement can live; what this means for an engineer.

The perimeter model

The classic design is a castle with a moat. A firewall sits at the edge of the corporate network. Traffic from the internet is filtered hard; traffic already inside is mostly allowed.

The firewall’s rules are written in terms of network location: “anything from 10.20.0.0/16 may reach the database on port 5432”. The address range stands in for “our own machines”. If a packet comes from the right place, it is trusted.

This was a reasonable bet when all servers sat in one building, all users sat at desks on the same network, and the number of systems was small. Location was a cheap and decent proxy for identity.

Definition · Perimeter model

Security based on a boundary between a trusted inside and an untrusted outside, with rules written in network locations. The trusted inside is sometimes called an implicit trust zone.

Keep the phrase implicit trust zone. NIST uses it, and the whole of zero trust is an effort to shrink it.

Where it breaks

The perimeter model fails in three ways that matter for an internal cloud.

First, attackers get in. A phishing email, a vulnerable web app, a stolen VPN credential, a compromised build dependency. Once one machine inside is controlled, the attacker inherits all the trust that location grants. The wall now protects the attacker.

Second, the inside is flat. If every internal machine can reach every other, one foothold leads everywhere. Moving from the first compromised machine to more valuable ones is called lateral movement. In many serious breaches the initial entry is the small part; the damage comes from quiet lateral movement afterwards.

Third, “inside” stopped being a place. Workloads run in containers that move between hosts every few minutes. Teams use remote laptops, partner networks, a second data hall, maybe a cloud tenant joined by a network link. An IP address now says little about what is behind it, and the next chapter shows why in detail.

The usual patch is to cut the inside into smaller zones with more firewalls: network segmentation, or at finer grain micro-segmentation. This helps. But the rules are still written in addresses, and someone must keep thousands of address rules aligned with a system that changes hourly. They drift. Drift becomes either outages or holes. Rule 4711 is drift that became a hole.

The zero trust idea

Zero trust starts from one assumption: assume breach. Treat the network as hostile, including your own. Then ask what you need to decide each access safely.

The answer is that every request should carry, or be checked against, three things:

  • Who is asking: an authenticated identity for the user, the device or the workload.
  • What is being asked for: a specific resource and action, not “the network”.
  • Whether policy allows it: evaluated now, using current information, not decided once at login.

Network location can still be one input. It is just never sufficient on its own. A request from inside the data hall with no valid identity is denied. A request from anywhere with a valid identity and matching policy is allowed.

Common mistake · “Zero trust means we trust nothing”

“Zero” is a slogan, not a literal claim. You still trust things: your certificate authority, your identity provider, your policy engine. The point is that trust is explicit, scoped and checked, rather than granted by default to anything that happens to be nearby.

BeyondCorp: the first large example

Google published a series of papers starting in 2014 describing BeyondCorp, its internal move away from a privileged corporate network. It is worth knowing because it showed the idea working at scale before the term zero trust became common.

The core moves were simple to state. Employees reached internal apps through an access proxy on the internet, not through a VPN. The proxy checked the user’s identity and the device’s identity and health against an inventory. Being on the office network gave no extra access.

Two lessons carry over to Granvik. Device or workload identity needs a reliable source of truth, an inventory. And the proxy in front of each service is where policy is enforced, which makes it a natural choke point to watch.

BeyondCorp was mostly about people reaching applications. Workload-to-workload traffic, which is most of what an internal cloud carries, came later in other designs and is the subject of chapters 6 and 9.

NIST SP 800-207 vocabulary

NIST SP 800-207, “Zero Trust Architecture”, published in 2020, is the reference most documents cite. It is not a product specification; it is a vocabulary and a set of principles. Learn its words and many other sources become easier to read.

The two basic nouns:

  • A subject is whoever or whatever is requesting access: a user, an application, a workload, a device. NIST uses “subject” so that machines and people are covered by the same rules.
  • A resource is what is being accessed: a service, an API, a database, a file share.

The decision machinery is split into logical parts:

  • The policy engine (PE) makes, and logs, the actual grant or deny decision.
  • The policy administrator (PA) executes that decision. It sets up or shuts down the path between subject and resource by commanding the enforcement point, and may generate a session-specific credential for the subject.
  • Together, PE and PA form the policy decision point (PDP). NIST notes that some implementations merge them into one service.
  • The policy enforcement point (PEP) sits in the path between subject and resource. It enables, monitors and terminates connections. It forwards requests to the PA and receives instructions and policy updates from it.

The PE decides using a trust algorithm: the logic that combines inputs into a decision. Inputs include the access request itself, subject identity and attributes, device or workload state, resource policy, and threat intelligence or activity logs. NIST distinguishes criteria-based algorithms (fixed rules must all hold) from score-based ones (a confidence score must cross a threshold). It also distinguishes singular evaluation, each request on its own, from contextual, which considers recent history.

NIST also separates the control plane, where PDP and PEP talk to each other, from the data plane, where application traffic flows. That split shows up in every product you will read about. SPIRE, Istio and OpenZiti all have a control plane that hands out identities and policy, and a data plane that carries traffic.

Definition · Control plane and data plane

The control plane decides and configures: it issues identities, distributes policy and tells enforcement points what to do. The data plane carries the application traffic and enforces what it was told.

The tenets, briefly

NIST lists seven tenets. Paraphrased:

  1. All data sources and computing services are resources.
  2. All communication is secured regardless of network location.
  3. Access is granted per session.
  4. Access is decided by dynamic policy, using identity, state and other attributes.
  5. The organisation monitors the integrity and security posture of its assets.
  6. Authentication and authorisation are dynamic and strictly enforced before access.
  7. The organisation collects information about assets, network and communications to improve its posture.

Tenet 2 is the one that kills location-based trust. Tenet 3 means that being allowed to reach one resource does not imply access to the next. Tenets 5 and 7 are observability requirements in disguise: logs, metrics and traces are part of the architecture, not an add-on.

Where enforcement can live

NIST describes several deployment variations for where the PEP sits. Recognising them helps you classify any product you meet.

  • Device agent / gateway: an agent on the subject’s machine talks to a gateway in front of the resource. Mesh sidecars and tunnelers look like this.
  • Enclave-based: one gateway guards a group of resources, an enclave. Easier for legacy systems, but trust inside the enclave is implicit again.
  • Resource portal: a single portal in front of resources, no agent on the subject. BeyondCorp’s access proxy is the classic case.
  • Application sandboxing: trusted applications run isolated on the subject’s device.

NIST also names three broad approaches: enhanced identity governance, micro-segmentation, and software-defined perimeters. Real systems mix them. An identity-based overlay like OpenZiti is a software-defined perimeter. Kubernetes NetworkPolicy is micro-segmentation. SPIFFE is identity governance for workloads.

What this means for an engineer

Zero trust turns a network problem into three engineering problems, and the rest of this book follows them.

  • Identity: every subject needs a verifiable, hard-to-steal identity. For workloads, that means keys and certificates issued automatically. Chapters 3 to 6.
  • Decision: policy must be written in terms of identities and attributes, kept in one place, and tested. Chapters 7 and 8.
  • Enforcement: something in the path must check every connection, without asking every team to rewrite their code. Chapters 9 and 10.

There is a cost. Every decision point is a dependency. If the PDP or the certificate authority is down, nothing new connects. Zero trust trades the risk of silent compromise for the risk of loud outages, and good designs work hard to make the second kind rare and local.

In the bubble model

The bubble model is a zero trust design whose unit of policy is a named scope rather than an address range. Mapping the NIST terms:

  • The subject is an atom’s workload identity, always tagged with the bubble it belongs to.
  • The resource is a service exported by a provider bubble.
  • The PEP is the bubble’s gateway, its “NAT”, plus optionally a proxy close to each atom. An edge-only design is NIST’s enclave-based variation; a per-atom proxy is closer to the device agent/gateway model.
  • The PDP reads bindings and trust declarations from Git and turns them into allow or deny.
  • The trust algorithm is criteria-based: is there a binding from consumer to provider, and does the caller’s identity match what the binding names?

Two problems show up already.

First, an edge-only gateway reintroduces an implicit trust zone inside each bubble. Atoms inside team-obs reach each other unchecked unless something enforces inside too. That may be acceptable for a small bubble, but it should be a declared choice, not an accident. The rule that trust stops at the lowest declared level helps, because it lets a team declare tighter trust deeper down where it matters.

Second, the PDP becomes critical infrastructure for all of Granvik. If bindings live in Git but decisions need a running service, you must decide what happens when that service is unreachable from one data hall. That question returns in chapter 11.

Synthesis

Synthesis

Rule 4711 was a perimeter rule: it trusted a location and let the location stand in for an identity. Zero trust removes that substitution. Every request is decided on an authenticated identity, a specific resource and explicit policy, and NIST SP 800-207 gives you the words for the parts that do it: a decision point that knows the policy and an enforcement point in the path. The price is that those parts become dependencies, so the rest of the book is as much about keeping them available as about making them strict.

Chapter 02

Networks in One Sitting

Packets, addresses, and why an address is a poor identity

A network moves packets between numbered addresses, and almost every tool on top of it (routing, NAT, firewalls, VPNs) exists to steer, rewrite or filter those numbers. Addresses are good at saying where to deliver a packet and bad at saying who sent it. Understanding exactly why is what makes the case for identity-based trust concrete rather than ideological.

Before you start

Chapter 1 argued that location should not be a reason to trust. This chapter shows what “location” means on a network, field by field, so the argument rests on mechanics rather than slogans.

The chapter in brief: packets and addresses; subnets and CIDR; TCP, UDP and ports; routing; NAT; DNS; firewalls and segmentation; VPNs, tunnels and overlays; why an IP address is a poor identity.

Packets and IP addresses

Everything on a network travels as packets: small chunks of data with a header in front. The header says where the packet came from and where it is going, like an envelope.

Those “from” and “to” fields are IP addresses. An IPv4 address is 32 bits, written as four numbers: 10.42.7.19. IPv6 addresses are 128 bits, written in hex: fd00:42::7:19. The ideas below hold for both.

The Internet Protocol (IP) promises very little. It tries to deliver each packet to the destination address. Packets may be lost, duplicated or arrive out of order. Crucially, nothing in IP checks that the source address is true. The sender writes it, and the network mostly believes it.

Subnets and CIDR

Addresses are handed out in blocks. A block is written in CIDR notation: an address plus how many leading bits are fixed. 10.42.0.0/16 means the first 16 bits are fixed, so it covers 10.42.0.0 to 10.42.255.255, about 65,000 addresses. A larger number after the slash means a smaller block. /24 is 256 addresses. /32 is exactly one.

A subnet is such a block assigned to one network segment, for example one VLAN, a virtual LAN carved out of shared switches, in a data hall. Machines in the same subnet can talk directly. To reach another subnet, they send packets to a gateway, usually a router.

Classic firewall rules are written in CIDR blocks. Kubernetes NetworkPolicy selects pods by label, but is enforced on pod addresses underneath. Trust decided by address is the habit zero trust tries to break.

TCP, UDP and ports

IP gets a packet to a machine. Transport protocols get it to the right program on that machine.

A port is a 16-bit number that identifies a conversation endpoint on a host. Servers listen on well-known ports: 443 for HTTPS, 5432 for PostgreSQL, 22 for SSH. Clients pick a random high port for their side.

The two main transport protocols:

  • TCP gives a reliable, ordered byte stream. It starts with a three-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK), resends lost data and closes cleanly. HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, PostgreSQL and most legacy protocols use TCP.
  • UDP just sends individual datagrams. No handshake, no delivery guarantee, low overhead. WireGuard, QUIC (the base of HTTP/3) and most DNS queries use UDP; DNS falls back to TCP for large answers.

One TCP connection is identified by a 5-tuple: source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port, protocol. Firewalls and NAT devices track connections by this tuple. Notice that every field is a number about location. None of them says who is talking.

Routing

Routing decides the next hop for each packet. Every host and router has a routing table: a list of CIDR blocks and where to send packets for each. The most specific matching block wins. A default route, 0.0.0.0/0, catches everything else.

Routers learn routes either from static configuration or from routing protocols such as BGP and OSPF. Inside a Kubernetes cluster, the CNI (Container Network Interface) plugin, the component that wires pods into the network, programs routes so pod subnets on different nodes can reach each other; chapter 9 covers that.

The key property: routers forward based on the destination address. They do not check the source. That is why source addresses can be forged on a shared segment, and why network reachability is not the same as permission.

NAT

Network Address Translation (NAT) rewrites addresses in packets as they pass through a device.

The common form, source NAT, lets many machines with private addresses share one public address. The NAT device replaces the source address and port on the way out, remembers the mapping, and reverses it on replies. Your home router does this. So does a Kubernetes node when a pod talks to something outside the cluster.

Destination NAT works the other way: traffic to one address and port is forwarded to some internal machine. Port forwarding and many load balancers work this way.

NAT has two side effects that matter here. It hides the original sender, so the receiver sees the NAT device’s address, not the real client. And it creates a one-way door: inside machines can start connections out, but outsiders cannot start connections in without an explicit forwarding rule.

Common mistake · “NAT is a security feature”

People often mistake NAT’s one-way door for security. It is a side effect of keeping a translation table, not a policy. Anything that creates a mapping from inside (a compromised host calling out, for instance) opens the door from the inside.

DNS

The Domain Name System (DNS) maps names to addresses. A client asks a resolver for loki.obs.svc.cluster.local; the resolver answers with one or more IP addresses.

Records have types. A and AAAA records give IPv4 and IPv6 addresses. CNAME records point one name at another. SRV records also give a port. Each answer has a TTL, how long it may be cached.

DNS is what lets things move: change the record, and clients follow after the TTL expires. But classic DNS is unauthenticated. A client cannot tell a real answer from a forged one unless DNSSEC or an encrypted, authenticated resolver path is used. Inside most clusters it is plain. So DNS gives you stable names, but a name resolved by DNS still proves nothing about who answers.

Firewalls and segmentation

A firewall filters packets by rules. A stateless firewall checks each packet alone. A stateful firewall tracks connections, so a reply to an allowed outbound connection is let back in automatically.

A typical rule: allow TCP from 10.42.0.0/16 to 10.60.3.12 port 5432. Everything not allowed is dropped.

Segmentation splits a network into zones with firewalls between them: a DMZ for internet-facing systems, an application zone, a database zone. Micro-segmentation pushes this down to individual workloads.

The problem is that the rules speak in addresses and ports while people think in services and teams. Someone must translate “the Loki ingesters may write to object storage” into address rules, and keep the translation true as things move. That translation is where errors, stale rules and change-board delays live.

VPNs, tunnels and overlays

A tunnel wraps one packet inside another. The inner packet keeps its own addresses; the outer packet carries it across some other network. When the outer packet arrives, the inner one is unwrapped and delivered.

A VPN is a tunnel with encryption and authentication, joining a remote machine or site to a network as if it were local. Traditional VPNs then hand out full network access: once connected, you are “inside”. That is the perimeter model again, extended over the internet.

An overlay network is a virtual network built from tunnels on top of an existing underlay. Kubernetes CNIs often use overlays such as VXLAN or Geneve, so pods get their own address space regardless of the physical network.

WireGuard is worth knowing as an idea. Each peer has a public/private key pair. A peer’s configuration lists other peers by public key and the inner addresses each may use. Packets are encrypted with keys derived from those pairs. So inside a WireGuard tunnel, an address is bound to a key: a packet claiming a source address is only accepted if it came from the peer whose key owns that address. Identity first, address second. Many mesh products build on this. Chapter 10 returns to identity-based overlays, where the tunnel carries a service identity rather than an address.

Why an IP address is a poor identity

An IP address fails as an identity for at least six reasons.

  1. It is not authenticated. The sender writes the source field. On a shared segment, or after a compromise, it can be forged.
  2. It is reused. Pods get addresses from a pool. When a pod dies, its address goes to the next pod, which may belong to another team. A rule written for the old pod now allows the new one.
  3. It is shared. Behind NAT, a load balancer or a node, many workloads appear as one address. You cannot tell them apart.
  4. It changes when things move. Move a service to another data hall and its address changes. Every rule naming it must change too.
  5. It describes location, not purpose. 10.42.7.19 says nothing about owner, role or team. Humans keep that mapping in spreadsheets and heads.
  6. It is the attacker’s inheritance. Compromise a machine and you inherit its address, and therefore everything address-based rules allow it.

An identity, by contrast, should be bound to something only the holder has, like a private key. It should name the workload’s purpose and owner, and stay the same when the workload moves. Chapters 3 to 6 build exactly that.

Addresses do not go away. Packets still need them to be delivered. The change is that addresses become plumbing, chosen and changed freely, while trust decisions use names backed by cryptography.

In the bubble model

“No IP addresses in any rule” follows directly from this chapter. Every reason in the list above is a way a rule would silently break or silently widen when a bubble moves or a pod restarts.

The bubble’s gateway is described as “the NAT”. That is a useful picture of the boundary, but be careful which properties you borrow. NAT’s one-way door is incidental, and NAT hides the original sender. If the gateway rewrites addresses, the provider must learn the caller’s identity some other way, for example from a client certificate or a forwarded token. Otherwise the provider sees only “the gateway”, and per-caller policy inside the provider becomes impossible.

Legacy plain TCP is the hard case. A PostgreSQL client knows only a host name and a port. The bubble model can keep that working by giving it a stable conceptual name that resolves, inside the consumer bubble, to a local tunnel endpoint. The tunnel, not the app, carries identity. Chapter 10 shows how tunnelers do this.

DNS is the natural carrier for conceptual names, but plain DNS is unauthenticated. A name is fine for finding a service. The certificate presented on connect must be what proves you reached the right one.

Finally, portability: if no rule contains an address, the same Git repository can bubble up in another data hall with a different address plan. Addresses become runtime state, never desired state.

Synthesis

Synthesis

Every field a network uses to deliver a packet is about location, and none of them is checked. Routers look only at the destination, NAT hides the sender, DNS answers can be forged, and pods inherit each other’s addresses. Address rules therefore drift quietly, usually in the wider direction. The fix is not to abolish addresses but to demote them to plumbing and to make trust depend on something a holder cannot fake: a private key. The next three chapters build that something.

What cryptography gives you

Cryptography provides a short list of guarantees. Name them precisely, because security arguments go wrong when they are blurred.

  • Confidentiality: only intended parties can read the data.
  • Integrity: any change to the data is detected.
  • Authenticity: you know who produced the data.
  • Non-repudiation: the producer cannot later deny it. This matters for audit, less for traffic.

Each tool below delivers some of these and not others. A hash alone gives integrity against accidents but not against attackers. Encryption alone gives confidentiality but not authenticity. Real protocols combine tools.

Hashes

A cryptographic hash function turns any input into a short, fixed-size output, the digest. SHA-256, for example, always produces 256 bits, usually shown as 64 hex characters.

Good hash functions have three properties:

  • Deterministic: the same input always gives the same digest.
  • One-way: from a digest, you cannot find an input that produces it.
  • Collision-resistant: you cannot find two different inputs with the same digest.

Change one bit of input and the digest changes completely. So a digest acts as a fingerprint. Container image references like sha256:9f86d0... are exactly this: the digest of the image content, so a tag cannot silently point to different bytes.

Common mistake · “A hash proves who made the data”

A plain hash does not prove who made the data. An attacker who changes the data can recompute the hash. To bind integrity to someone, you need a key.

Symmetric encryption

Symmetric encryption uses one secret key for both encrypting and decrypting. Anyone with the key can do both. AES and ChaCha20 are the common algorithms.

It is fast, and it is what actually protects your bytes in TLS, WireGuard and disk encryption. Modern modes are authenticated encryption (AEAD, such as AES-GCM): they encrypt and also attach a tag that detects tampering. So you get confidentiality and integrity together.

A close relative is the MAC (message authentication code). A common one, HMAC, is a hash mixed with a shared key. Only key holders can produce a valid MAC, so it proves the message came from one of them.

The weakness is in the word “shared”. Both sides need the same secret. How do they get it without someone in the middle copying it? And with many parties, a shared key cannot tell you which of them produced a message. Asymmetric cryptography solves both problems.

Asymmetric keys

Asymmetric or public-key cryptography uses a key pair: a private key kept secret by its owner, and a public key that can be given to anyone. They are mathematically linked, but the private key cannot be derived from the public one.

Common algorithm families: RSA, and elliptic-curve schemes such as ECDSA with P-256 and Ed25519. Elliptic-curve keys are much smaller for the same strength, which is why most new systems prefer them.

A key pair supports two operations that matter here:

  • Signing: the private key produces a signature; anyone with the public key can verify it.
  • Key agreement: two parties combine their key pairs to derive a shared secret. This is covered below.

Asymmetric operations are slow compared with symmetric ones. So protocols use them briefly, to authenticate and agree on a key, then switch to symmetric encryption for the data.

Digital signatures

A digital signature proves that the holder of a specific private key approved specific data.

Signing works like this: hash the data, then transform the digest with the private key. Verifying takes the data, the signature and the public key, and answers yes or no. If one bit of the data changed, or a different key signed it, the answer is no.

A signature gives integrity and authenticity at once. Unlike a MAC, the verifier holds no secret, so anyone can check, and only one party could have produced it.

Signatures are everywhere in this book. A certificate is a public key plus some names, signed by a certificate authority. A JWT is a JSON payload signed by an identity provider. A signed Git commit or container image proves who produced it. In each case, ask two questions: which key signed this, and why do I trust that key? The second question leads to chains of trust, the subject of chapter 4.

Note what a signature does not say. It proves possession of a key, not who the human or workload behind the key is. Binding a key to a name is someone else’s job, and that binding is where most real attacks aim.

Key exchange

Key exchange lets two parties agree on a shared secret over a channel an eavesdropper can watch, without ever sending the secret.

The classic method is Diffie-Hellman, today usually its elliptic-curve form, ECDHE. Each side generates a temporary key pair, sends the public half, and combines the other side’s public key with its own private key. Both arrive at the same secret. An eavesdropper sees only public halves and cannot compute it.

Two details matter.

First, plain key exchange is anonymous. A man in the middle can run one exchange with each side and relay between them. To stop that, at least one side signs the exchange with a long-term private key the other side trusts. In TLS 1.3 the signature covers a hash of the whole handshake, including both key shares. That is what a server certificate is for in TLS, and in mutual TLS both sides do it. Chapter 5 walks through it.

Second, the “E” in ECDHE means ephemeral: fresh key pairs per session, thrown away afterwards. This gives forward secrecy. If a server’s long-term key is stolen next year, recorded traffic from today still cannot be decrypted, because the session keys are gone.

Definition · Forward secrecy

The property that stealing a long-term key later does not let an attacker decrypt traffic recorded earlier, because the session keys came from ephemeral key pairs that no longer exist.

Why private keys must never move

Everything above comes down to this: whoever holds a private key is that identity, as far as any verifier can tell. There is no second check. So the security of an identity equals the security of its private key.

That leads to a design rule. Generate the private key where it will be used, and never let it leave. Do not create keys centrally and ship them. Do not put them in Git, in config maps, in environment variables, or in tickets.

The right pattern looks like this:

  1. The workload, or something on its host, generates a key pair locally.
  2. It sends only the public key, inside a certificate signing request (CSR), to an issuer.
  3. The issuer checks that the requester deserves the name it asks for, then signs a certificate.
  4. The private key stays put. When it expires, the workload makes a new one.

Short lifetimes help a lot. A key that is valid for one hour and is replaced automatically limits the damage of a leak to that hour. Chapter 4 explains why this often beats revocation.

Common mistake · “We just copy the key to the second cluster”

Every time a design copies a private key (to a backup, a second cluster, a “shared service account”), the identity now lives in two places, and you can no longer tell them apart. Treat that as a design smell, not a convenience.

HSMs and TPMs

The strongest way to keep a key from moving is to put it in hardware that will not hand it out.

A hardware security module (HSM) is a hardened device that generates and stores keys and performs operations with them. You send it data to sign; it returns the signature. The private key never leaves in plain form. HSMs protect the most valuable keys, such as a root certificate authority.

A Trusted Platform Module (TPM) is a small chip on a server’s motherboard, or a firmware equivalent. It can hold keys bound to that one machine. It also records measurements of what booted: hashes of firmware, bootloader and kernel. A TPM can sign those measurements, a step called attestation, so a remote party can check what a machine is running before trusting it.

TPMs often ship with a manufacturer-issued endorsement key and can carry a device identity certificate, which the IEEE 802.1AR standard calls a DevID. SPIRE’s tpm_devid node attestor uses such a certificate to prove “this is physical server X”. That gives a hardware root for the identity chain, which matters for the bottom turtle problem in chapter 6.

Both have limits. They protect the key, not the decision to use it. A compromised process on the host can still ask the TPM to sign. Hardware raises the bar against theft and copying, not against misuse.

In the bubble model

The bubble model’s promise, “certificates issued by or for the bubble and rotated automatically”, is a key-management design, and this chapter sets its rules.

Each atom should generate its own private key and send only a CSR. The bubble then decides whether the atom deserves the name it asks for. That decision is the hard part. Something must vouch that this pod really is gv/team-obs/loki before anything signs. Chapter 6 shows how SPIRE does that with attestation.

“Issued by the bubble” needs care. If every bubble runs its own signing key, every bubble holds a valuable private key. Where does it live? An HSM per bubble is costly. A shared HSM with per-bubble keys is plausible. A per-bubble key in a Kubernetes secret is weak. The alternative is one issuer that signs names for many bubbles, with policy limiting which names each requester may get. That shifts trust from many keys to one policy, and chapter 4’s name constraints become relevant.

Portability creates a direct tension. “The same GitOps repository bubbles up in another data hall” must not mean the same private keys appear there. Git holds names, bindings and the public parts of trust anchors. Private keys are runtime state, generated fresh in each place. If a design needs to copy a signing key between halls, treat that as the decision to examine first.

For physical machines and VMs, a TPM gives the bottom of the chain: a key that cannot be copied off the box.

Synthesis

Synthesis

Each cryptographic tool proves something narrow. A hash detects change, a MAC proves membership of a group of key holders, a signature proves that one particular key approved particular data, and a key exchange creates a secret nobody else saw, provided someone signs it. None of them proves who is behind a key. That binding is the job of certificates, and its strength rests entirely on the private key staying where it was born. Design every flow so that keys are generated in place, live briefly and never travel.

Who may sign “this key belongs to this name”, and what happens when it stops being true

A certificate is a signed statement: “this public key belongs to this name, for this purpose, until this date.” Everything else in public key infrastructure exists to answer two questions about that statement: who is allowed to sign it, and what happens when it stops being true.

Before you start

The chapter in brief: the problem a certificate solves; anatomy of an X.509 certificate; extensions; roots and intermediates; trust stores; chain validation; revocation; short-lived certificates; the root key ceremony; name constraints; running your own PKI.

The problem a certificate solves

A public key on its own proves nothing about who holds it. Chapter 3 showed that a signature proves possession of a private key. It does not tell you whose key that is.

A certificate binds a public key to an identity. Someone you already trust checks the binding and signs it. That someone is a certificate authority (CA).

So when a server shows you its certificate, you are trusting the CA that signed it, not the server. The server then proves it holds the matching private key.

Anatomy of an X.509 certificate

Almost every certificate you will meet uses the X.509 format (version 3). It is a structured record, signed by the issuer. The fields that matter most:

  • Subject: who the certificate is about, written as a distinguished name (DN), for example CN=loki, O=Granvik.
  • Subject Alternative Name (SAN): the names that actually count today: DNS names, IP addresses, email addresses or URIs.
  • Issuer: the DN of the CA that signed it.
  • Validity: notBefore and notAfter timestamps.
  • Subject public key: the key being vouched for.
  • Serial number: unique per issuer, used for revocation.
  • Extensions: extra rules, described below.
  • Signature: the issuer’s signature over all of the above.

A trimmed view of a workload certificate might look like this:

Subject:         O=Granvik
Issuer:          CN=team-obs intermediate CA
Validity:        2026-10-30 09:00 to 2026-10-30 10:00 UTC
SAN:             URI:spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki/ingester
Key Usage:       Digital Signature
Ext Key Usage:   TLS Web Server, TLS Web Client
Basic Constraints: CA:FALSE
Common mistake · “The Common Name is what clients check”

The Common Name (CN) is legacy. Modern TLS clients check the SAN and ignore CN for hostname matching. Workload identity systems (chapter 6) put their identity in a URI SAN.

Extensions: what the key may be used for

Extensions limit what a certificate can do. They are how a CA says “I vouch for this key, but only for this”.

  • Basic Constraints: CA:TRUE means this certificate may sign other certificates. CA:FALSE marks a leaf (end-entity) certificate. An optional path length limits how many CAs may sit below it.
  • Key Usage: low-level permissions such as digital signature or certificate signing (keyCertSign).
  • Extended Key Usage (EKU): higher-level purposes such as serverAuth and clientAuth. A certificate with only serverAuth should be rejected when presented by a client.
  • Name Constraints: on a CA certificate, the set of names it may issue for. More on this below.

These are enforced by the software that validates the certificate, not by the certificate itself. A careless validator that skips a check gives you no protection from it.

CAs, roots and intermediates

A root CA has a self-signed certificate. Nobody vouches for it; you trust it because you chose to install it. That act of installation is the real root of trust.

Roots are too valuable to use daily. So a root signs one or more intermediate CAs, and the intermediates sign leaf certificates. The root’s private key can then be kept offline, often in an HSM (chapter 3), and used only a few times a year.

This gives you a certificate chain: leaf, signed by intermediate, signed by root.

Intermediates also limit damage. If an intermediate key leaks, you revoke that intermediate. The root, and every other branch, stays intact.

Trust stores and trust bundles

A trust store is the set of root certificates a piece of software accepts as anchors. Your operating system, your browser, Java and .NET each have one, and they are not always the same.

In service-to-service systems the same idea is usually called a trust bundle: the set of CA certificates that a workload accepts for a given domain. SPIFFE uses this term (chapter 6).

The key mental model: a trust store defines your world. Any certificate chaining to any root in it is accepted. Adding a root is a large decision, because you trust everything that root will ever sign.

Chain validation, step by step

When software receives a certificate, it runs path validation. In outline:

  1. Build a chain from the leaf up to a root in the trust store, using the intermediates the peer sent.
  2. Check each signature with the public key of the certificate above it, and that each issuer name matches the subject name above it.
  3. Check each certificate is within its validity period.
  4. Check every non-leaf certificate has CA:TRUE and respects path length.
  5. Check name constraints on every CA in the chain.
  6. Check key usage and EKU fit the current purpose.
  7. Check revocation status. RFC 5280 includes this step, but many systems skip it or soft-fail, as the next section explains.
  8. Finally, check the leaf’s name matches what you expected: the hostname you dialled, or the identity you require.
Common mistake · “A valid chain means the right service”

Step 8 is the one most often forgotten. A perfectly valid chain to a trusted root only proves “some CA I trust vouched for this key”. It does not prove “this is the service I wanted”. Authorization starts after validation.

Revocation, and why it mostly fails

Sometimes a certificate must stop being trusted before it expires. A key leaked, a server was decommissioned, or the CA made a mistake. This is revocation.

Two classic mechanisms exist:

  • Certificate Revocation List (CRL): the CA publishes a signed list of revoked serial numbers. Clients download it periodically.
  • Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP): the client asks the CA’s responder about one certificate. OCSP stapling lets the server fetch the answer and attach it to the handshake.

Both have the same weakness. If the client cannot reach the list or responder, it must choose. Fail closed breaks everything when the CA’s endpoint is down. Fail open means an attacker who blocks the check wins. Most clients historically failed open, so revocation was weaker than it looked.

Short-lived certificates instead

The modern answer is to make certificates expire so quickly that revocation hardly matters. If a certificate lives for one hour, a leaked key is useful for at most one hour.

This only works if issuance and rotation are fully automatic. Nobody renews hourly certificates by hand. The workload, or an agent next to it, must fetch a new certificate well before the old one expires.

The trade-off moves rather than disappears. Short lifetimes make the CA a live dependency: if issuance is down longer than a certificate’s lifetime, workloads fail. Keep lifetimes longer than plausible CA outages, and run the CA highly available. Clocks matter too. A workload with a skewed clock may reject valid certificates.

Example · One-hour certificates at Granvik

Granvik’s platform team settles on one-hour workload certificates, renewed when half the lifetime has passed. Their issuing service has a recovery target of 20 minutes. If it fails just after a renewal, workloads have about 30 minutes of valid certificate left, which covers the recovery target with a margin. If they had chosen 15-minute certificates, a 20-minute outage would start breaking connections. The lifetime is a function of how fast they can repair the CA, not a security number picked in isolation.

The root key ceremony

Because the root private key defines your world, its creation is treated as an event. A key ceremony is a scripted, witnessed procedure. Several people are present, each step is recorded, and the key is generated inside an HSM so it never exists in plain form. Often the key, or the authority to use it, is split so that several key custodians must cooperate. The result is a root certificate, a signed intermediate or two, and a written record anyone can audit.

You do this rarely, and you want to prove years later that it was done right.

Name constraints

Name constraints are an extension on a CA certificate. They list permitted and excluded name spaces. A CA constrained to the DNS subtree obs.granvik.internal cannot issue a valid certificate for portal.granvik.internal, even if it tries.

This is how you delegate a CA to a team without handing them the whole world. The validator rejects any leaf whose names fall outside the constraints of any CA above it.

Three caveats matter. First, constraints apply per name type: DNS, IP, email, URI and DN are separate. A CA constrained only for DNS names can still issue unconstrained URI names. Second, RFC 5280 says a URI constraint applies only to the host part of the URI. For a SPIFFE ID like spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki, that is granvik.internal, never the path after it. Third, support for URI name constraints is uneven across TLS libraries. Test the specific libraries you depend on before relying on them.

Running your own PKI

An internal cloud needs a private PKI: roots that only your systems trust. Public CAs will not issue for internal names, and you would not want them to.

The parts you have to operate are:

  • The offline root and its ceremony.
  • Online issuing intermediates, ideally with keys in HSMs.
  • An issuance API with authentication of requesters.
  • Distribution of trust bundles to every client.
  • Rotation of the intermediates and, eventually, the root.

The last one is underrated. Root rotation works by overlap: trust both, issue from the new, then remove the old. Plan it before you need it.

In the bubble model

The bubble hierarchy maps naturally onto a CA hierarchy. One offline root for Granvik, and an issuing intermediate per top-level bubble such as team-obs, constrained to that bubble’s name space. A leaf certificate for each atom carries its identity in a URI SAN. This expresses “certificates are issued by the bubble” literally.

It is attractive, but there are honest problems.

Nesting depth. If every nested bubble gets its own intermediate, gv > team-obs > loki becomes a root, two intermediates and a leaf: a four-certificate chain, longer with deeper nesting. Every handshake carries and checks it. A flatter design issues per top-level bubble and puts the nesting into the name, not the chain.

Name constraints on URIs. A URI name constraint cannot stop the team-obs CA issuing for team-portal when both share one trust domain: the constraint sees only the host part, and the bubble lives in the path. So the issuance system must enforce it, which moves trust from mathematics to software you operate.

Names in certificates. A bubble rename or reorganisation changes every leaf name below it. With short-lived certificates that is just the next rotation. But every policy that matched the old name must change too. This is the first sign that stable synthetic IDs and human names will need to be separated.

Trust is wider than the chain. A shared root means every workload can validate every other workload’s certificate. That is fine: validation is not authorization. The bubble’s bindings, not the PKI, decide who may call whom.

Synthesis

A certificate moves the question “whose key is this?” onto a CA you chose to trust, and a trust store is the list of such choices. Validation proves that some trusted CA vouched for a key; only the final name check proves it is the key you wanted, and only authorization decides whether its holder may act. Revocation was never reliable, so modern designs issue certificates that expire before a leak matters, which in turn makes the CA a service you must keep running. For nested scopes, put the hierarchy in names rather than in long chains, and remember that URI name constraints cannot see the path.

Chapter 05

TLS and Mutual TLS

A protected connection, and what it never proves

TLS turns a certificate into a protected connection: it proves the server holds the key named in its certificate, agrees fresh keys for the session, and encrypts what follows. Mutual TLS makes the client prove the same thing. What it never proves is that the caller should be allowed to do what it asks.

Learning objectives

  • Walk through the TLS 1.3 handshake and say where authentication happens
  • List what a completed handshake proves to each side, and what it does not
  • Explain SNI and ALPN and why proxies care about them
  • Describe mutual TLS and the three points about it that are easy to miss
  • Compare termination, passthrough and origination, and what each one does to identity
  • Diagnose the common ways mTLS goes wrong in practice
Essential question

After a successful mTLS handshake, what does the server know, and what does it still have to decide?

Before you start

Chapter 3 described signed key exchange, and chapter 4 described how a certificate binds a key to a name. TLS is the protocol that puts the two together on every connection.

The chapter in brief: what TLS gives you; the TLS 1.3 handshake; forward secrecy; what is and is not proven; SNI and ALPN; mutual TLS; termination, passthrough and origination; what proxies see; TLS inside tunnels; where mTLS goes wrong.

What TLS gives you

Transport Layer Security (TLS) sits on top of TCP and below your application protocol. HTTPS is HTTP inside TLS. Its job has three parts:

  • Confidentiality: nobody on the path can read the data.
  • Integrity: nobody on the path can change it undetected.
  • Authentication: at least one side proves its identity with a certificate.

The current version is TLS 1.3. TLS 1.2 is still widely deployed and acceptable when configured well. Older versions should be off.

The TLS 1.3 handshake, step by step

The handshake does two things at once: it agrees a shared secret, and it authenticates the server. In TLS 1.3 this takes one round trip.

ClientHello. The client sends supported cipher suites, a random value and a key share: its half of an ephemeral key exchange (chapter 3). It also names the server it wants (SNI) and the protocols it speaks (ALPN).

ServerHello. The server picks parameters and sends its own key share. Both sides can now compute the same secret. Everything after this point is encrypted.

Certificate and CertificateVerify. The server sends its certificate chain. Then it signs a hash of the handshake so far with its private key. This signature is the actual proof. A copied certificate is useless without the private key.

Finished. Each side sends a message keyed on the full handshake. If anything was tampered with, the values will not match and the connection dies.

The client validates the chain and checks the name, as in chapter 4, when the server’s Certificate arrives and before it sends its own Finished. If either check fails, it aborts.

Forward secrecy

The session keys come from ephemeral key shares that are thrown away afterwards. The certificate key is used only to sign.

This gives forward secrecy: if the server’s private key leaks next year, recorded traffic from today still cannot be decrypted. Every full TLS 1.3 handshake has it; only an optional resumption mode without a fresh key share lacks it. Some TLS 1.2 configurations had none.

What is and is not proven

After a successful handshake the client knows:

  • The server holds the private key for a certificate that chains to a trusted root.
  • The certificate names what the client expected, if the client checked.
  • Nobody between them can read or change the stream.

The client does not know:

  • That the server is honest or uncompromised.
  • That the server is the last hop. It may be a proxy.
  • Anything about what the server will do with the data.

The server, in plain TLS, knows nothing about the client at all.

SNI: choosing a certificate before encryption

One IP address and port often host many services. The server must choose which certificate to present before any encryption exists.

Server Name Indication (SNI) solves this. The client puts the target hostname in the ClientHello, in clear text. Load balancers and proxies use it to route connections without decrypting them.

Because SNI is visible, anyone on the path learns which name you are connecting to. Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) addresses this but is not yet universal. For internal design, assume SNI is visible and usable for routing.

ALPN: choosing the protocol

Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation (ALPN) lets the client list protocols it can speak inside TLS, such as h2 (HTTP/2) or http/1.1. The server picks one.

It matters for proxies and meshes. gRPC requires HTTP/2, and a misconfigured proxy that negotiates HTTP/1.1 breaks it. Some systems also use custom ALPN values to signal a tunnelling protocol.

Mutual TLS

In mutual TLS (mTLS) the server also demands a certificate from the client. It sends a CertificateRequest in its encrypted flight, right after EncryptedExtensions. It may list the CAs it accepts, but in TLS 1.3 that list is optional. The client answers with its own Certificate, CertificateVerify and Finished.

Now both sides hold a verified identity for the other. Every connection carries cryptographic proof of who is calling, independent of IP address.

Three points are easy to miss:

  • The server must decide what to do with the identity. mTLS proves “this is team-obs/grafana”. Whether grafana may call this service is an authorization decision (chapter 8).
  • Both sides need trust bundles. The server needs the client’s CA, and the client needs the server’s.
  • The identity is the connection’s, not the request’s. Many requests on one connection share it. A user behind the client is invisible to mTLS (chapter 7).

Termination, passthrough and origination

When a proxy sits in the path, it handles TLS in one of three ways. The choice decides who sees what.

Mode What the proxy does What it sees What the backend sees
Termination Completes the handshake with its own certificate, then forwards in plaintext or new TLS Plaintext; can apply L7 policy The proxy’s identity, unless the proxy forwards the caller’s
Passthrough Reads only the ClientHello (mostly SNI) and forwards encrypted bytes untouched Names and sizes, not content The real client certificate
Origination Starts TLS on behalf of a client that speaks plain TCP or HTTP Plaintext from the local app The proxy’s or sidecar’s certificate

Termination gives you L7 policy, meaning rules about the application protocol rather than just addresses and ports. Passthrough keeps the handshake end to end. Origination is how a sidecar, a helper proxy running next to the application, or a gateway gives legacy applications mTLS without changing them. The application believes it is talking plaintext to a neighbour; the wire carries mTLS.

A common chain is origination at the caller’s side and termination at the receiver’s side. Each application speaks plaintext locally, and the network between them carries mTLS between two proxies.

What proxies see

Every terminating proxy is a point where data is plaintext and identity is replaced. That makes it both useful and dangerous. Useful, because it is where you can enforce policy, log requests and add tracing. Dangerous, because compromise of the proxy exposes everything passing through it. The backend must also trust the proxy’s claims about the original caller.

When a proxy forwards the original client identity, it usually puts it in a header.

Common mistake · “The identity header says who called”

A header is only trustworthy if the backend accepts it solely from that proxy, over an authenticated connection, and the proxy strips any copy the client sent. Otherwise any client can set the same header and become whoever it likes.

TLS inside tunnels

TLS can run inside another tunnel, and tunnels can themselves be TLS. Both patterns appear in modern platforms.

A tunnel carries one protocol inside another. WireGuard and IPsec carry IP packets. An HTTP CONNECT tunnel carries a TCP stream inside an HTTP request. Istio ambient’s HBONE (chapter 9) is a tunnel of the latter kind: HTTP/2 CONNECT inside mTLS, between per-node proxies.

For each layer, ask who authenticated to whom. An outer mTLS tunnel proves whatever identity its certificate carries, usually the proxy’s own. Istio’s ztunnel deliberately presents the certificate of the workload it carries traffic for instead. Either way, the outer layer says nothing about the payload inside, which might be its own end-to-end TLS. Neither layer automatically inherits the other’s identity.

Where mTLS goes wrong in practice

Most mTLS failures are not broken cryptography. They are missing checks and operational gaps.

  • Accepting any valid client. A server that trusts a broad CA and checks nothing else lets every certificate holder in. Always match the client’s identity against an expected set.
  • Skipping name checks on the client side. Clients that disable hostname or identity verification “to make it work” accept any server with a trusted certificate. That turns mTLS into encrypted trust-everyone.
  • Trust bundles out of date. When a CA rotates and one side lacks the new certificate, handshakes fail with vague errors such as “unknown CA”. Automate and observe bundle distribution.
  • Certificates expiring silently. Short-lived certificates make expiry a constant event. Export remaining lifetime as a metric and alert well before zero.
  • Long-lived connections. Authentication happens once per handshake, and resumed sessions reuse it. A revoked or rotated identity may linger on old connections. Bound connection age.

In the bubble model

mTLS is the natural enforcement mechanism at a bubble’s edge. The gateway terminates mTLS from callers, reads the caller’s identity, checks it against bindings, and forwards inward. Legacy plain TCP and HTTP services fit through origination: a proxy or tunneler on each side adds mTLS so the application never sees it. This is what “mTLS at the edges may be enough” means in practice.

Honest problems follow.

Identity is replaced at the gateway. Behind a terminating gateway, the backend sees the gateway, not the atom that called. If policy inside the bubble needs the original caller, the gateway must pass it on, and the backend must trust only the gateway to say so. Passthrough keeps the real identity but loses L7 policy.

Edge-only mTLS leaves the inside flat. If traffic inside the bubble is plaintext, the bubble is a small perimeter. Chapter 1’s lateral movement problem returns at a smaller scale. That may be an acceptable trade for legacy workloads, but it should be a stated decision.

Nested gateways multiply hops. If team-obs and loki each have an edge, a call may pass two terminating proxies. Each is a plaintext point and a latency cost.

Connection identity, not request identity. A binding enforced by mTLS says which bubble calls. It cannot say on whose behalf. Delegated identity needs tokens (chapter 7) carried inside the mTLS connection.

Synthesis

Synthesis

TLS proves that the peer holds the private key behind a certificate your trust store accepts, and protects the bytes in between. Mutual TLS gives the server the same proof about the client, which finally makes “who is calling?” a cryptographic fact instead of an address. But the identity belongs to the connection, it is replaced at every terminating proxy, and it says nothing about permission. The rest of the book adds what TLS leaves out: who issues workload identities (chapter 6), whose behalf a call is on (chapter 7) and whether it is allowed (chapter 8).

Chapter 06

Workload Identity with SPIFFE and SPIRE

A name and a credential for every workload, with no secret planted first

SPIFFE is a standard for giving every running workload a name and a short-lived credential it never had to be configured with. SPIRE is a system that implements it by checking facts about the machine and the process, rather than trusting a secret someone put there. The hard part is not issuing certificates; it is deciding, with no prior secret, who deserves one.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the secret zero (bottom turtle) problem and how attestation addresses it
  • Read a SPIFFE ID and distinguish X.509-SVIDs from JWT-SVIDs
  • Describe the Workload API and why the workload presents no credential to call it
  • Trace node attestation, workload attestation and registration entries in SPIRE
  • Explain what federation gives you, and what SPIFFE deliberately does not do

Essential question

Before a workload has any credential at all, what could possibly prove who it is?

Before you start

Chapter 3 said the issuer must check that the requester deserves the name it asks for, and chapter 4 showed what the issued certificate looks like. This chapter is about that check.

The chapter in brief: secret zero; SPIFFE and SPIRE; the SPIFFE ID; SVIDs; trust bundles; the Workload API; SPIRE’s architecture; node and workload attestation; registration entries; rotation; federation; what SPIFFE does not do; operating SPIRE.

The problem: secret zero

A workload needs a credential to prove who it is. Somebody has to give it that credential. How does the giver know it is handing it to the right workload?

The usual answer is a pre-shared secret: an API key in an environment variable, a password in a vault. But then the workload needs a credential to fetch the secret from the vault. This regress is called secret zero, or the bottom turtle problem, after the “turtles all the way down” joke.

SPIFFE’s answer is attestation: identify a workload by verifiable properties of where and how it runs, not by something it holds. The book Solving the Bottom Turtle, by SPIFFE contributors, is the long-form explanation and is worth reading after this chapter.

SPIFFE and SPIRE

SPIFFE (Secure Production Identity Framework For Everyone) is a set of specifications. It defines the identity format, the credential formats and an API for delivering them. It is a CNCF project.

SPIRE (the SPIFFE Runtime Environment) is one implementation. Others exist; for example, Istio issues SPIFFE-format identities from its own CA. You can adopt the SPIFFE formats without running SPIRE.

The SPIFFE ID

A SPIFFE ID is a URI naming a workload:

spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki/ingester

It has two parts. The trust domain (granvik.internal) is the authority: everything in it shares one root of trust. The path (/team-obs/loki/ingester) names the workload within that domain.

The specification gives the path no meaning. Its structure is your design choice. Common conventions encode environment, namespace, service account or team. The specification allows an ID per instance, but in practice a SPIFFE ID usually names a role or service, and many replicas share it.

SVIDs: the credentials

An SVID (SPIFFE Verifiable Identity Document) is the proof that a workload holds a SPIFFE ID. There are two kinds.

An X.509-SVID is an ordinary X.509 leaf certificate (chapter 4). The SPIFFE ID sits in the SAN as the only URI. It is used for mTLS (chapter 5). Any TLS library can use it; peers just need to check the URI SAN instead of a hostname.

A JWT-SVID is a signed JSON Web Token (chapter 7). Its payload looks like:

{
  "sub": "spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/grafana",
  "aud": ["spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki"],
  "exp": 1793350800
}

The sub is the SPIFFE ID; aud names the intended receiver and is mandatory. JWT-SVIDs travel through proxies and load balancers that terminate TLS, where an X.509-SVID cannot. The cost is replay: anyone who steals the token can use it until it expires. Prefer X.509-SVIDs where you can, and keep JWT-SVIDs short-lived and narrowly addressed.

Trust bundles

A trust bundle is the set of public keys that validate SVIDs from one trust domain: CA certificates for X.509-SVIDs and signing keys for JWT-SVIDs. A workload receives the bundle for its own domain alongside its own SVID.

The bundle changes over time as CAs rotate. That is why it is delivered dynamically, not baked into images.

The Workload API

The Workload API is how a workload gets its SVID and bundles. It is a local endpoint, typically a Unix domain socket, exposed by an agent on the same node.

The important property: the workload presents no credential to call it. Instead the agent inspects the caller. The kernel tells the agent the calling process ID. From that the agent learns the user, the container, the pod and more.

The API is streaming. The agent pushes new SVIDs before old ones expire and new bundles when they change. A well-behaved client library swaps them in without restarts. For applications that cannot do this, a helper sidecar can write files to disk instead.

SPIRE architecture

SPIRE has two components.

The SPIRE Server is the CA and the source of truth. It holds the signing keys (optionally via an HSM or an upstream CA), stores registration entries, and attests agents.

The SPIRE Agent runs on every node. It attests itself to the server, caches the SVIDs its node’s workloads are entitled to, attests workloads locally and serves the Workload API.

Node attestation

Node attestation answers: is this agent really running on the machine it claims? The agent sends evidence that only a genuine node could produce, and the server verifies it through a plugin, a node attestor.

Each substrate has its own source of evidence:

  • k8s_psat: a projected service account token from Kubernetes, verified against the cluster API.
  • tpm_devid: a device identity key held in the machine’s TPM (chapter 3).
  • x509pop: proof of possession of an existing machine certificate.
  • azure_msi and azure_imds: signed identity documents from Azure.
  • aws_iid and gcp_iit: the equivalents on AWS and Google Cloud.
  • join_token: a one-time token an operator generates. Simple, but it is a pre-shared secret: a turtle you placed by hand.

The pattern is the same everywhere. Some lower layer (the cloud provider, the Kubernetes control plane, a hardware chip) vouches for the node. You are choosing which turtle to stand on.

Workload attestation

Workload attestation answers: which workload is this process? The agent queries local authorities through workload attestors. The Kubernetes attestor asks the kubelet about the pod. The Unix attestor reads user and group IDs and the binary path. Others read container runtime metadata.

The output is a set of selectors: facts like k8s:ns:team-obs and k8s:sa:loki-ingester.

Registration entries

A registration entry maps selectors to a SPIFFE ID. It says: a workload matching these selectors, on a node matching this parent ID, gets this identity.

SPIFFE ID: spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki/ingester
Parent ID: spiffe://granvik.internal/spire/agent/k8s_psat/ocp-hall-a/...
Selectors: k8s:ns:team-obs
           k8s:sa:loki-ingester
X509-SVID TTL: 1h

All selectors must match. This is where policy about identity lives: an administrator, or an automated controller, decides what facts earn what name. In Kubernetes, the SPIRE Controller Manager can create entries from custom resources, which makes them GitOps-friendly.

Common mistake · “Entries are just configuration”

Registration entries are the most sensitive configuration in the system. Whoever can write an entry can mint any identity. Protect write access to entries as you would protect a CA key.

Rotation

SVIDs are short-lived, often an hour or less. The agent renews them in the background, well before expiry, and pushes them over the Workload API. Bundles rotate the same way when the server’s CA key rolls over.

This is chapter 4’s short-lived certificate model made routine. Revocation is largely replaced by “stop renewing”: delete the registration entry, and the identity disappears within one lifetime.

Federation

A trust domain is one root of trust. Sometimes two must trust each other: two data halls run separately, or a partner organisation runs its own SPIRE.

SPIFFE federation lets trust domains exchange bundles. Each publishes its bundle at a bundle endpoint, and the other fetches it periodically. The first fetch needs something to trust: either the endpoint’s ordinary web certificate, or a bundle copied over once by an operator. Workloads then receive bundles for foreign domains too and can validate their SVIDs.

Federation is about validation, not permission. It lets granvik.internal verify that a caller really is spiffe://partner.example/billing. Whether billing may call anything is still your authorization decision.

SPIFFE identities also reach outside SPIFFE. For example, a JWT-SVID can be exchanged for a Microsoft Entra token through workload identity federation (chapter 10), so an on-prem workload calls Azure without a stored secret.

What SPIFFE does not do

  • No authorization. SPIFFE tells you who is calling. It never says whether the call is allowed. That belongs to a policy engine or mesh (chapters 8 and 9).
  • No user identity. A SPIFFE ID names a workload. A user on whose behalf it acts is carried separately, in OAuth tokens (chapter 7).
  • No network enforcement. SPIRE issues credentials; it does not block packets. Something else must refuse connections without valid SVIDs.
  • No meaning in the path. As noted, the structure of IDs is yours. Two teams with different conventions inside one trust domain will confuse every policy.

Operating SPIRE

SPIRE becomes tier-zero infrastructure. If the server is unavailable longer than SVID lifetimes, workloads lose their identities and calls start failing.

So run the server highly available, with a replicated datastore. Agents cache SVIDs and keep serving during short server outages, which buys time. Choose lifetimes with your realistic recovery time in mind.

The server’s signing keys can chain to an existing PKI through an upstream authority plugin. That lets an offline root from chapter 4 sit above SPIRE.

In the bubble model

SPIFFE fits the bubble model’s demand for an identity at every atom almost exactly. One trust domain for Granvik, and paths following the bubble hierarchy: spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/loki/ingester. Registration entries map OpenShift namespaces and service accounts to bubbles. VMs and physical machines join through tpm_devid or x509pop. Rotation is automatic. Azure links through the Azure attestors or Entra federation.

The honest problems:

Path as hierarchy is only a convention. “Calls from team-obs are OK” must be prefix matching in your policy engine, applied identically at every enforcement point.

Names versus synthetic IDs. If the path contains team-obs, a reorganisation renames every identity and breaks every policy referencing it. If the path contains a synthetic ID like /b/7f3a/w/91c2, identities survive moves but become unreadable. You likely need both: synthetic IDs in the SVID, names in a catalogue that policy authors use.

One trust domain or many. One domain spanning data halls is simple but is one shared root. A domain per hall, federated, isolates failures but makes “the same repository bubbles up elsewhere” harder: the domain name appears in every ID.

Entries are the real trust boundary. Whoever can write registration entries for team-obs can mint its identities. Bubble ownership must therefore include, and be limited to, entry authority for its own subtree.

One CA key, not one per bubble. A SPIRE server signs all its SVIDs with one CA key. Chapter 4’s per-bubble intermediates would need a nested SPIRE server per bubble. The boundary is usually better enforced in entries and policy.

The bottom turtle is per substrate. Each attestor trusts a different lower layer. A bubble spanning OpenShift and bare metal is only as strong as its weakest attestor; a join_token there quietly undermines the rest.

Synthesis

Synthesis

SPIFFE gives workloads names and short-lived credentials in standard formats; SPIRE issues them by attestation, trusting a lower layer (the Kubernetes control plane, a TPM, a cloud provider) to vouch for the node and local facts to identify the process. That dissolves secret zero, but it does not remove trust: it moves it into the attestors you pick and the registration entries you write. Treat entry write access as the power to mint identities, choose lifetimes with your recovery time in mind, and remember that SPIFFE says who is calling and nothing about whether they may.

Why people need a different mechanism

Workloads get identity from attestation: the platform looks at the process and vouches for it. People cannot be attested that way. They log in with passwords, passkeys or smart cards, at a browser, from devices you do not control.

So human identity lives in a separate system, the identity provider (IdP). At Granvik that is Entra ID, with Keycloak in front of some applications. The IdP authenticates the person once, then hands out tokens that other systems can check without talking to the person again.

The protocols for this are OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749) and OpenID Connect (OIDC), a thin identity layer on top of OAuth. They are easy to misuse because they solve two different problems that look alike.

OAuth 2.0: delegated access, not login

OAuth answers one question: may this application call that API, with these permissions?

The specification names four roles:

  • Resource owner: the person whose data or rights are involved.
  • Client: the application that wants access.
  • Authorization server (AS): issues tokens. Usually part of the IdP.
  • Resource server (RS): the API that accepts tokens.

The client never sees the password. It gets an access token, a credential the RS accepts. A refresh token lets the client get new access tokens without bothering the user again. Access tokens are short-lived, typically minutes to an hour.

A grant type is a recipe for obtaining a token. Two matter today.

Authorization code with PKCE

This is the flow for a person using an application. The client redirects the browser to the AS. The user logs in there, not in the client. The AS redirects back with a one-time authorization code. The client then swaps the code for tokens over a direct back-channel call.

PKCE (RFC 7636, pronounced “pixie”) closes a gap: someone who steals the code from the redirect could redeem it. With PKCE, the client invents a random secret, sends only its hash at the start, and must present the secret itself when redeeming the code. A stolen code is useless without it.

Common mistake · “Implicit and password grants are fine for internal apps”

The older implicit and password grants are deprecated in the current best-practice guidance, internal or not. If you see them in a design, that is a finding.

Client credentials

When no person is involved, a service authenticates as itself and gets a token for its own rights. This is the client credentials grant. The service proves itself with a client secret, or better, a private key or certificate.

This is where workload identity and OAuth meet. Instead of a stored secret, the service can present its SPIFFE certificate (via mutual TLS) or a JWT-SVID as a client assertion. That removes the last long-lived password from the picture.

OpenID Connect: who logged in

OAuth deliberately says nothing about who the user is. An access token means “the bearer may do X”, not “this is Anna”. Using an access token as proof of login is a classic bug.

OIDC adds the ID token: a statement from the IdP, addressed to the client, saying who authenticated, when, and how. The client consumes it to start a session. It should never be sent to an API as an access credential.

OIDC also standardises discovery (a well-known URL describing the IdP’s endpoints) and a JWKS endpoint publishing the IdP’s public signing keys. Any service can fetch those keys and verify tokens locally.

Anatomy of a JWT

Most tokens you will meet are JSON Web Tokens (JWT, RFC 7519). A JWT is three base64url parts separated by dots: header, payload, signature. The header names the signing algorithm and key ID. The signature covers the first two parts.

A decoded access token payload might look like this:

{
  "iss": "https://idp.granvik.example/realms/gv",
  "sub": "f3c1a9e2-anna",
  "aud": "loki-query-api",
  "azp": "grafana",
  "scope": "logs.read",
  "exp": 1793353200,
  "iat": 1793352900
}

The claims carry the meaning:

  • iss: who issued and signed it. The verifier must trust this issuer.
  • sub: the subject, the identity the token is about.
  • aud: the audience, the service the token is meant for.
  • exp and iat: expiry and issue time.
  • scope: what the client is allowed to do. Scopes are coarse labels, not fine-grained policy.
  • azp: the authorized party, the client the token was issued to. Keycloak adds it; the standard JWT access token profile, RFC 9068, uses client_id for this instead.

A verifier must check the signature against the issuer’s published keys, the expiry, and that aud names itself.

Common mistake · “The signature is valid, so the token is for us”

Skipping the audience check is the most common serious mistake. A token issued for service A, replayed against service B, would otherwise work.

A JWT is readable by anyone holding it. Signing gives integrity, not confidentiality. Put nothing secret in claims.

Bearer tokens and sender constraint

A plain access token is a bearer token: whoever holds it can use it, like cash. If it leaks into a log or a compromised proxy, it is usable until it expires.

RFC 8705 fixes this for mutual TLS. When the client gets a token over an mTLS connection, the AS records a SHA-256 thumbprint of the client’s certificate in a cnf (confirmation) claim:

"cnf": { "x5t#S256": "bwcK0esc3ACC3DB2Y5_lESsXE8o9ltc05O89jdN-dg2" }

The RS then accepts the token only over an mTLS connection presenting that same certificate. A stolen token without the matching private key is worthless. This is called a certificate-bound or sender-constrained token. RFC 8705 also defines how a client authenticates to the AS with its certificate.

DPoP (RFC 9449) achieves similar binding at the application layer, for clients that cannot do mTLS end to end.

The confused deputy problem

Delegation creates a specific danger. A confused deputy is a program with legitimate authority that is tricked into using it on behalf of someone who lacks that authority.

Example · A report that reads too much

A reporting service at Granvik has broad read access to all teams’ logs. A user from team A asks it for a report and passes a query that names team B’s logs. The service fetches them with its own rights. The service did nothing outside its permissions, yet team A just read team B’s data.

The root cause is that the downstream API saw only the service’s identity, not the user’s. Two fixes exist. Either the service checks the user’s rights itself, which spreads policy everywhere. Or the downstream API sees both identities and decides. Token exchange enables the second.

Token exchange: RFC 8693

OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange (RFC 8693) defines a grant where a client hands the AS one token and asks for another. The request carries:

  • subject_token: the token representing the party on whose behalf the request is made, usually the user. It is required, together with a subject_token_type saying what kind of token it is.
  • actor_token (optional, with its own type): the token representing the party that will act, usually the calling service.
  • audience or resource: where the new token will be used.
  • requested_token_type: for example an access token or a JWT.

The AS applies its own policy and returns a new token scoped to the next hop. Two patterns result.

Impersonation: the new token simply says sub is the user. The downstream service cannot tell that a middleman was involved. Simple, but it hides the deputy.

Delegation: the new token keeps the user as sub and adds an act (actor) claim naming the service:

{
  "iss": "https://idp.granvik.example/realms/gv",
  "sub": "f3c1a9e2-anna",
  "aud": "loki-query-api",
  "act": { "sub": "spiffe://granvik.internal/team-obs/report-svc" }
}

Now the API knows both who asked and who is acting. Act claims can nest to record a chain of hops. The outermost act is the current actor. The RFC says a consumer must decide only on the top-level claims and that current actor; nested, earlier actors are informational only.

The may_act claim works in the other direction. Placed in the subject token, it says which party is permitted to act for the subject. The AS can check it before issuing either a delegated or an impersonation token.

Note the audience narrowing: each token is good for exactly one next service. That defeats replay and limits the blast radius of a leak.

Keycloak and Entra ID in this picture

Keycloak is an open-source IdP you can run on OpenShift. It implements OAuth and OIDC, and can broker logins to an upstream IdP such as Entra. Recent releases support standard RFC 8693 token exchange, but delegation with an actor token and act claim only reached preview status in Keycloak 26.8. Preview features are not meant for production, so check the version you run.

Entra ID implements OAuth and OIDC but uses its own delegation flow, called On-Behalf-Of (OBO). It is conceptually close to token exchange, but the request format differs and its tokens do not carry an RFC 8693 act claim in the same way. Entra also supports workload identity federation: an external workload presents a token from a trusted issuer, such as a SPIFFE JWT-SVID, and receives an Entra token without any stored secret.

The practical consequence: an on-prem platform will likely have two token worlds, internal and Entra. Decide early which issuer each API trusts, and where translation happens.

Pitfalls to look for in designs

  • Tokens with no audience, or a shared audience across many services.
  • APIs that accept ID tokens as access tokens.
  • Long-lived bearer tokens stored in config or environment variables.
  • Services forwarding the user’s original token downstream unchanged. This is impersonation by accident, with an over-broad audience.
  • Authorization decisions based on scope alone, with no check of who the subject is.

In the bubble model

Bubbles give atoms workload identity; this chapter adds people. A binding between team-obs and team-permits says which bubble may call which. It does not say which person is behind a call. Both questions must be answerable at the edge.

The clean fit is delegation tokens with an actor claim. Anna’s request enters through Grafana in team-obs. When the report service calls loki in another bubble, it exchanges Anna’s token for one with sub Anna, act its own SPIFFE ID, and aud the target. The bubble edge can then check the binding on the actor and pass the user to the service’s own policy.

Certificate-bound tokens (RFC 8705) suit the model well. The atom already has an mTLS certificate from its bubble, so binding tokens to it costs little and makes leaked tokens useless. One catch: the binding is to one exact certificate, so automatic rotation invalidates outstanding tokens. Token lifetimes must stay shorter than certificate lifetimes, or clients must fetch new tokens after each rotation.

Problems this creates:

  • Who runs the token service? Token exchange needs an AS that understands bubbles and bindings. Entra does not. You may need an internal AS, for example Keycloak once its delegation support matures, whose exchange policy is generated from the same GitOps repository as the bindings.
  • Names in tokens. Audiences must be the conceptual service names, not hostnames, or the “bubble up in another data hall” goal breaks.
  • Legacy services. Plain TCP apps cannot read tokens. For them, the bubble edge can only enforce the binding, not the user. Be explicit that user-level authorization stops there.
  • Non-transitivity. A delegation chain crosses bubbles. If X calls Y, and Y calls Z on Anna’s behalf, Z sees act chains. Z’s binding must be with Y; the nested history must not quietly grant X anything.

Synthesis

Synthesis

OAuth issues tokens that say what a client may do; OIDC adds a separate token that says who logged in, meant only for the client. Every verifier must check issuer, signature, expiry and, above all, audience. Bearer tokens are cash; binding them to a certificate makes theft useless. When a service acts for a person, token exchange with an act claim lets the next service see both, which is the cure for the confused deputy. The cost is an authorization server in the path of every cross-service call, and in practice two token worlds to keep apart.

Authorization and Policy as Code

Explicit, versioned, testable rules for who may do what

Knowing who is calling is only half the job; something must then decide whether to allow it, and that decision should come from explicit, versioned, testable rules rather than from code scattered across services. The hard design questions are where decisions are made, how rules combine across levels, and how you prove they do what you think.

Before you start

Chapters 5 to 7 produced identities: a certificate for the workload and a token for the person. This chapter turns identities into decisions.

The chapter in brief: authentication is not authorization; RBAC, ABAC and ReBAC; PDP and PEP in practice; deny by default; policy as code with OPA and Cedar; where the PDP gets its facts; hierarchies and inheritance; testing policy.

Authentication is not authorization

Authentication (authN) establishes who someone is. Authorization (authZ) decides what they may do. Certificates, SPIFFE IDs and tokens are authentication. They produce an identity and some attributes, nothing more.

Mixing them up causes real failures. “Any request with a valid company certificate is allowed” is authentication pretending to be authorization. It turns every compromised workload into a key to everything.

A useful way to phrase every authorization question: may this principal perform this action on this resource, in this context? Principal is the caller. Action is the verb, such as read or POST /query. Resource is the thing touched. Context is everything else: time, device posture, source bubble, how the caller authenticated.

Three models for writing rules

RBAC. Role-based access control groups permissions into roles and assigns roles to principals. “Members of obs-admins may delete dashboards.” Kubernetes RBAC is the example you know: Roles, ClusterRoles and RoleBindings. RBAC is easy to read and audit. Its weakness is role explosion. When access depends on more than job function, you end up with roles like obs-reader-loki-prod-team-a, and nobody can say what anyone has.

ABAC. Attribute-based access control decides from attributes of principal, resource and context. “Allow if the caller’s team equals the resource’s owning team and the environment is not production.” One rule covers many cases. ABAC is flexible but harder to review. You must trust that attributes are correct and come from authoritative sources. A wrong team label on a namespace silently changes who has access.

ReBAC. Relationship-based access control decides from a graph of relationships. “Anna may view this dashboard because she is a member of team-obs, which owns the folder that contains it.” Google described this in its Zanzibar paper; OpenFGA and SpiceDB are open-source systems in that style. ReBAC fits nested ownership well, because access follows the structure of the organisation. The cost is a separate relationship store that must be kept consistent and available.

Real systems mix all three. Roles for coarse grouping, attributes for context, relationships for ownership.

Decision and enforcement: PDP and PEP in practice

Chapter 1 introduced NIST’s terms. Here they become concrete.

The policy enforcement point (PEP) sits in the request path and can block it: a proxy, a gateway, a middleware in the service. The policy decision point (PDP) evaluates the rules and returns allow or deny. The policy administration point (PAP) is where rules are authored and distributed; in practice, a Git repository and a pipeline. A policy information point (PIP) supplies extra data, such as group memberships.

Common mistake · “PAP and PA are the same thing”

PAP and PIP come from the older XACML standard, not NIST 800-207. Do not confuse the PAP, where policy is authored, with NIST’s policy administrator, which acts on decisions at runtime.

The PDP can run as a central service or as a library next to each PEP. Central is simpler to update but adds latency and a dependency. Local is fast and survives outages, but every copy must receive policy updates. Most mesh designs choose local evaluation with policy pushed from a central control plane.

A key rule: the PEP must fail closed. If it cannot reach the PDP or cannot parse the policy, it denies.

Deny by default

Default deny means nothing is allowed unless a rule explicitly allows it. It is the core of zero trust and the opposite of most traditional networks.

Every new dependency then needs an explicit allow. That friction is the point: the allow rule is where the dependency gets documented, reviewed and owned.

It also changes failure modes. A missing rule causes an outage, which is visible. A too-broad rule causes nothing visible until it is exploited. Prefer the visible failure.

Many systems also support explicit deny, which overrides any allow. Deny-overrides is the safe combining rule: if anything says no, the answer is no.

Policy as code

Policy as code means rules are written in a machine-readable language, stored in version control, reviewed like code, tested in CI, and deployed by pipeline. You probably already do this with Kubernetes manifests via GitOps. Policy is the same discipline applied to decisions.

OPA and Rego

Open Policy Agent (OPA) is a general-purpose PDP from the CNCF. Policies are written in Rego, a declarative query language. OPA receives a JSON input document, evaluates the rules, and returns a JSON decision.

package bubble.authz

default allow := false

allow if {
    input.action == "read"
    input.principal.bubble == "team-obs"
    startswith(input.resource.path, "/loki/")
}

OPA is used as an Envoy external authorizer, as Kubernetes admission control through Gatekeeper, and embedded in services. Its strength is generality: any JSON in, any decision out. The cost is that Rego is unfamiliar, and generality makes policies hard to analyse automatically.

Cedar

Cedar is a policy language created by AWS and released as open source. It is narrower by design. Every policy is a permit or forbid over principal, action, resource and conditions.

permit (
    principal in Bubble::"team-obs",
    action == Action::"read",
    resource in Service::"loki"
);

Cedar evaluates as default deny, and any matching forbid overrides every permit. Policies are checked against a schema of entity types. Because the language is restricted, tools can analyse policies, for example to prove that one policy set never allows more than another.

Choose OPA for breadth and existing integrations, Cedar for readable rules with strong guarantees about how they combine.

Where the PDP gets its facts

A policy is only as good as its inputs. The rule “caller’s bubble equals team-obs” assumes the PEP knows the caller’s bubble reliably. In a mesh, that comes from the verified certificate, not from a header the caller set.

Treat every input by its source. Identity from mTLS or a verified token is trustworthy. Attributes from a PIP, such as group membership or resource ownership, are as trustworthy as the system that stores them. Anything the client sends unverified is not an input at all.

Hierarchies and inheritance

Nested scopes raise one question: what does a rule at a parent level mean for children? There are two answers, and mixing them causes most trouble.

Granting inheritance: a permission at the parent flows down. If gv grants read, every child inherits read. Convenient, but one broad grant high up silently widens access everywhere below.

Restricting inheritance: the parent sets a ceiling. Children can grant within it but never exceed it.

AWS Organizations service control policies (SCPs) are the standard precedent for the restricting kind. Accounts sit in a tree of organizational units under a root. An SCP attached anywhere in the tree limits the maximum permissions of all accounts beneath it. An SCP never grants anything; the account’s own policies must still allow the action. An explicit deny at any level wins. SCPs do not apply to the management account or to service-linked roles: even a ceiling has exempt principals.

The effective permission is the intersection of every level. This is a powerful property: a central team can guarantee “nobody below here can ever do X” without knowing what is below.

The lesson for any nested design: let parents restrict and set defaults, and keep grants and ceilings as separate rule types that combine differently.

Testing policy

Policy is code, so test it like code.

  • Unit tests: for each rule, assert specific allow and deny cases. OPA has opa test; Cedar has validation and test tooling in its SDKs.
  • Negative tests first: write the cases that must be denied. Cross-team access, expired identities, missing attributes.
  • Diff on change: in CI, run a fixed corpus of requests against old and new policy and show what changed. A reviewer should see “this change newly allows 14 requests”.
  • Decision logs: PDPs can log every decision with its inputs. Ship those to your log store and trace them with OpenTelemetry. They let you answer “why was this denied?” in an incident, and catch unused allow rules.
  • Shadow mode: run a new policy alongside the old, log its decisions without enforcing, compare, then switch.

Gherkin scenarios fit naturally: “Given a caller in team-permits, when it reads loki, then it is denied.”

Tip

Granvik’s auditor would have had an answer in minutes if rule 4711 had been policy as code with a decision log: the rule would have had an owner and a review history, and the log would have shown that nothing had used it for years.

In the bubble model

The bubble model is, at heart, an authorization model. Bubbles are ReBAC: access follows nesting and ownership. Bindings are explicit grants, so the whole system is default deny with bindings as the only allow.

The bubble model’s rule that trust inheritance stops at the lowest level where it is declared is related to the SCP lesson but not the same. SCPs intersect every level. The bubble rule is override: a grant declared on a provider bubble covers what lies beneath it until a lower bubble declares its own, and then only the lower one applies. Nesting itself passes down names, owners and defaults, never blanket access. Parents may also set SCP-style ceilings (“nothing in gv may bind to the internet without review”), which intersect. This avoids the trap where a rule on gv opens everything.

The edge gateway is the PEP. A Cedar or OPA PDP beside it evaluates principal (calling atom or bubble), action, resource (the exported service) and context (the delegated user from chapter 7).

Problems:

  • Two sources of truth. Kubernetes RBAC, OpenShift roles, Entra groups and bubble bindings will overlap. Decide which system owns which question, or they will disagree.
  • Atom-level exceptions. If trust can be granted per atom, policies multiply. Keep that rare and make it visible in review.
  • Non-transitivity must be tested. Write a test corpus proving that adding a binding X to Y changes no decision involving Z. The diff-on-change check above does exactly this.
  • Names, not addresses. Policies must reference bubble and service names. Any rule that mentions an IP is a bug.

Synthesis

Synthesis

Authentication gives you an identity; authorization is a separate decision, best made by a PDP from explicit rules and enforced by a PEP that fails closed. Default deny makes missing rules loud and turns every allow into documentation. Rules belong in version control with tests, diffs and decision logs, in a language whose combining rules you understand. For nested scopes, keep two kinds of rule apart: grants, which are local, and ceilings, which intersect. The bubble model is exactly that kind of authorization model, with bindings as its only grants.

The chapter in brief: the flat pod network; Services, DNS and namespaces; NetworkPolicy; ingress and egress; what a service mesh adds; the sidecar model; Istio ambient mode with ztunnel and waypoints; AuthorizationPolicy; egress through the mesh; multitenancy on OpenShift.

The flat pod network

Kubernetes makes one networking promise: every pod gets its own IP address, and every pod can reach every other pod without NAT. A pod is one or more containers sharing a network identity.

The promise is implemented by a CNI plugin (Container Network Interface). On current OpenShift the default is OVN-Kubernetes. It builds an overlay network across nodes so pod addresses work cluster-wide.

Pod IPs are ephemeral: a restarted pod usually gets a new one, so IPs cannot be identities here.

Services, DNS and namespaces

A Service gives a stable name and virtual IP in front of a changing set of pods, chosen by label selector. Clients call loki-gateway.team-obs.svc.cluster.local; DNS resolves it; the node forwards to a healthy pod.

A namespace is a scope for names and for RBAC. On OpenShift, a project is a namespace with extra metadata and a default template. Namespaces are not network boundaries by default. A pod in team-a can reach a Service in team-b freely.

Each pod also runs as a ServiceAccount. That is the identity Kubernetes knows about, and the one meshes turn into certificates.

NetworkPolicy: firewall rules by label

A NetworkPolicy restricts which pods may talk to which, using labels and namespaces rather than addresses. Once any policy selects a pod for a direction, ingress or egress, traffic in that direction not explicitly allowed is dropped.

kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: allow-from-grafana, namespace: team-obs }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: loki } }
  ingress:
  - from:
    - namespaceSelector: { matchLabels: { team: obs } }
      podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: grafana } }

NetworkPolicy works at layers 3 and 4: addresses and ports. It does not prove identity. Labels are resolved to IPs by the CNI, so anyone who can spoof or reuse an address, or label a pod, can pass. Nothing is encrypted.

Namespace owners write NetworkPolicy, which is a problem for platform-wide guarantees. OVN-Kubernetes supports AdminNetworkPolicy (ANP) and BaselineAdminNetworkPolicy (BANP), cluster-scoped rules a platform team sets. OpenShift declared them generally available in 4.16, though the upstream API is still v1alpha1. ANP rules are evaluated first and can Allow, Deny or Pass; a matching Allow or Deny is final, while Pass hands the decision to namespace rules. BANP applies only where namespaces say nothing. This resembles the “parent sets ceilings and defaults” idea from chapter 8, with one difference: an ANP Allow grants, which an SCP never does.

Ingress and egress

Ingress is traffic entering the cluster. OpenShift uses Routes, served by the HAProxy-based router, and also supports the Kubernetes Ingress resource. The router can terminate TLS, re-encrypt to the pod, or pass it through: the three modes from chapter 5.

Gateway API is the newer Kubernetes standard for this. It separates roles: the platform team owns a GatewayClass and Gateway (the listener), application teams attach HTTPRoute objects (or TCPRoute, still experimental) to it. That split of ownership is the main reason to prefer it.

kind: HTTPRoute
metadata: { name: loki-query, namespace: team-obs }
spec:
  parentRefs: [{ name: shared-edge, namespace: platform-gw }]
  hostnames: ["loki.obs.granvik.example"]
  rules:
  - backendRefs: [{ name: loki, port: 3100 }]

The application team writes the route; the platform team decides which namespaces may attach to its Gateway.

Egress is traffic leaving the cluster. OpenShift offers EgressIP, giving a namespace a fixed source address so outside firewalls can recognise it, and EgressFirewall to restrict destinations. Both still speak in IP terms, which outside systems often require.

What a service mesh adds

A service mesh puts a proxy in every network path between workloads. The proxies are configured by a central control plane; the proxies themselves are the data plane.

The mesh gives three things NetworkPolicy cannot:

  • Identity: each workload gets an X.509 certificate. Istio encodes the ServiceAccount as a SPIFFE ID like spiffe://cluster.local/ns/team-obs/sa/loki.
  • mTLS everywhere: proxies authenticate each other and encrypt traffic, without application changes.
  • Policy on identity: rules refer to SPIFFE IDs or ServiceAccounts, not labels or IPs.

The control plane, istiod, acts as the CA. It is a SPIFFE-compatible issuer, though not SPIRE itself; Istio can also be configured to take identities from SPIRE.

The sidecar model

Classic Istio injects an Envoy sidecar into every pod. It works, but every pod pays memory and CPU, and upgrades require restarting every workload.

Istio ambient mode

Ambient mode removes the sidecar and splits the mesh into two layers. OpenShift Service Mesh 3.2 made ambient mode generally available; 3.3 added FIPS support for it, and multicluster ambient is a technology preview.

ztunnel: the secure layer 4 overlay

ztunnel (zero-trust tunnel) runs once per node as a DaemonSet. It intercepts traffic from ambient-enrolled pods on that node, and holds certificates for each of those pods’ identities.

ztunnel handles only layer 4: who is connecting to whom, encrypted. It cannot see HTTP paths or methods. Between nodes it uses HBONE (HTTP-Based Overlay Network Environment): the original TCP stream is carried inside an HTTP CONNECT tunnel over mTLS, on port 15008. Each tunnel carries the source and destination workload identities.

Enrolling is a label: istio.io/dataplane-mode: ambient on a namespace. Pods are not restarted or modified. A legacy app speaking plain TCP gets mTLS and identity for free.

Waypoint: optional layer 7

A waypoint is an Envoy proxy deployed as needed, typically one per namespace or per service account. ztunnel routes traffic to it when a destination is configured to use one, via the istio.io/use-waypoint label.

The waypoint understands HTTP and gRPC. It applies path- and method-based authorization, retries, traffic splitting, and request-level telemetry. Waypoints are declared as Gateway API Gateway resources, so they are owned like any other gateway.

Note what each proxy sees. ztunnel handles the raw bytes, since it does the encrypting, but never parses them: it acts on identities, addresses and ports, not paths or headers. A waypoint terminates the mTLS tunnel, so it sees the plaintext request, including headers and tokens. That makes waypoints high-value targets: they must be owned, patched and monitored like any gateway. If the application itself uses TLS inside the tunnel, the waypoint sees only encrypted bytes and cannot apply layer 7 rules.

AuthorizationPolicy

Istio’s AuthorizationPolicy is the mesh’s PEP configuration. It allows or denies requests by source identity, namespace, and, at layer 7, by method, path or header. A DENY policy overrides ALLOW, matching the deny-overrides rule from chapter 8.

kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata: { name: loki-readers, namespace: team-obs }
spec:
  targetRefs:
  - { kind: Service, group: "", name: loki }
  action: ALLOW
  rules:
  - from:
    - source:
        principals: ["cluster.local/ns/team-permits/sa/report-svc"]
    to:
    - operation: { methods: ["GET"], paths: ["/loki/api/v1/query*"] }

In ambient mode placement matters. A policy using only layer 4 fields (principals, namespaces, ports) can be enforced by ztunnel with a workload selector. A policy using HTTP fields must target a waypoint, as above.

Common mistake · “Ztunnel will apply my HTTP rules”

If ztunnel ends up enforcing a layer 7 policy, it skips the HTTP rules. An ALLOW policy then matches nothing, so traffic is denied. A DENY policy with only HTTP rules blocks nothing, so traffic is allowed. Review placement carefully; the second case fails open without any error.

Istio can also validate JWTs with RequestAuthentication and use their claims in AuthorizationPolicy. That is how the user from chapter 7 reaches mesh policy.

Egress through the mesh

Default Istio lets workloads call anything outside. Two controls tighten this. A ServiceEntry registers an external destination by hostname so the mesh can apply policy to it. An egress gateway or egress waypoint forces outbound traffic through one controlled proxy, where it can be logged, authorised and given TLS origination.

Allowing only registered destinations makes egress default deny.

Multitenancy on OpenShift

The OpenShift building blocks for hosting many teams on one cluster:

  • Projects with RBAC: teams administer their own namespaces, not others’.
  • Security context constraints (SCCs): limit what pods may do on the host.
  • Project templates: new namespaces start with default NetworkPolicies, quotas and labels.
  • AdminNetworkPolicy: platform-wide guardrails namespaces cannot override.
  • Service mesh: identity and mTLS across tenants.

This is soft multitenancy. Tenants share nodes, a kernel, and the control plane. It suits teams in one organisation who are not hostile to each other. It does not suit mutually untrusted tenants without dedicated nodes or clusters.

In the bubble model

OpenShift maps to bubbles with some force. A namespace can be an atom-holding bubble, or several namespaces can belong to one. ServiceAccounts give atoms identity; ambient mesh turns them into certificates without touching pods, which matters for legacy TCP.

The waypoint is a natural bubble edge for HTTP. AuthorizationPolicy targeting it is where a binding becomes enforceable, and Gateway API’s ownership split matches provider-owned exports. AdminNetworkPolicy fits the parent-sets-ceiling rule.

Problems:

  • Trust domain and names. Istio’s default SPIFFE IDs encode namespace and ServiceAccount, not bubble. If a bubble moves namespaces or clusters, every principal string changes. You need a mapping layer, or SPIRE-issued IDs that name bubbles directly.
  • Nesting is not native. Kubernetes namespaces are flat. Nested bubbles must be expressed through labels and generated policy.
  • One cluster is one mesh. Bindings across data halls or to VMs need multicluster mesh, still maturing in ambient, or an overlay (chapter 10).
  • Layer 4 only, without waypoints. Plain TCP bindings can check who, not what. That may be enough at the edge, but say so explicitly.
  • IPs creep back at egress. EgressIP and outside firewalls still want addresses. Keep them in the platform layer, never in bubble rules.

Synthesis

Synthesis

A Kubernetes cluster starts flat: any pod can reach any other, and NetworkPolicy can only filter by labels that resolve to addresses. A service mesh adds what policy needs: a certificate per workload, mTLS on every hop, and rules written in identities. Ambient mode does it without touching pods, with ztunnel for identity at layer 4 and waypoints only where HTTP-level rules are needed, and the placement of each AuthorizationPolicy decides whether it is enforced at all. Inside one cluster that is enough to make a namespace a trust boundary; beyond one cluster it is not, which is the next chapter.

Beyond One Cluster: Overlays and Federation

One identity story across clusters, VMs, bare metal and the cloud

A service mesh secures traffic inside a platform, but Granvik spans several platforms: OpenShift clusters, VMs, bare metal, some Azure. Two families of tools stretch identity across that gap. Overlays build a new, identity-addressed network on top of whatever IP network exists. Federation lets separate identity systems accept each other’s credentials without merging. Most real designs need both, plus an explicit way to export a service from one owner and import it into another.

The problem: many substrates, one identity story

Chapter 9 ended inside one Kubernetes cluster. There, the mesh knows every pod, the control plane hands out certificates, and policy is a resource in the same API. That neat picture breaks the moment a call leaves the cluster.

A VM in another data hall has no service account. A physical database host has no sidecar. An Azure function lives in someone else’s identity system. Each of these is a substrate: a place where workloads run, with its own way of starting things and its own idea of who they are.

You need three things to make a call between substrates trustworthy:

  • A workload identity that means the same thing everywhere.
  • A transport that carries authenticated, encrypted traffic between substrates without relying on IP-based firewall rules.
  • A policy that decides who may reach what, expressed in identities, not addresses.

The rest of this chapter looks at tools for each, and at the precedents for exporting services across ownership boundaries.

Identity-based overlays

An overlay network is a network built on top of another one. Packets of the overlay travel inside packets of the underlay, as chapter 2 described for tunnels. An identity-based overlay goes one step further: endpoints are addressed and authorised by cryptographic identity, and the underlying IP addresses become plumbing.

OpenZiti in depth

OpenZiti is an open-source zero-trust overlay. It is worth studying closely because its vocabulary maps unusually well onto bindings between bubbles.

The controller is the management plane. It holds the PKI, the list of identities, services and policies, and an API. It does not carry application traffic.

Edge routers form the data plane. They are a mesh of routers that relay encrypted traffic. A router can be public, accepting inbound connections, or private, making only outbound connections. Routers choose paths across the mesh themselves, so a link failing in one data hall does not need a firewall change.

An identity is any participant: a user, a device, a workload, a tunneler. Each identity has an X.509 certificate issued by the controller. It gets that certificate through enrollment: an administrator creates the identity, the controller produces a one-time token (a JWT), and the endpoint uses it once to generate a key pair locally and obtain its certificate. The private key never leaves the endpoint.

A service is a named thing you can connect to, such as loki-push. It is not an IP and port. A service has configs that tell edge software how to intercept and how to host it; you will see names like intercept.v1 and host.v1 in the docs.

Service policies connect identities to services. They come in two types:

  • Dial: these identities may connect to these services.
  • Bind: these identities may host these services.

Policies select identities and services by role attributes, written with a hash: #team-obs, #loki-hosts, or the built-in #all. You tag identities and services with attributes and write policies against the tags. This is attribute-based selection, as in chapter 8.

Two more policy types decide which routers an identity or service may use: edge router policies and service edge router policies. Posture checks can add conditions on the endpoint, such as operating system version or whether a process is running.

Applications reach the overlay in one of two ways. An SDK embeds the overlay in the application itself; the app has its own identity and never opens a listening port. A tunneler is a separate process that intercepts traffic for unmodified applications, on the client side, the host side, or both. Tunnelers are what make legacy TCP work.

Nebula and WireGuard meshes

Two other designs are worth knowing by name. Nebula, from Slack, gives each host a certificate that names its overlay IP and its groups. Firewall rules on each host are written against groups, and lighthouses help hosts find each other. Note that the overlay IP is part of the identity; that is a different trade-off from OpenZiti.

WireGuard-based meshes such as Tailscale, or Headscale, an independent open-source reimplementation of its control server, build full-mesh WireGuard tunnels between devices. Access rules are written with users and tags. They are excellent for connecting machines; they are thinner on per-service policy and on hosting dark services.

Dark services and legacy apps

A dark service has no open inbound port on any network. The hosting side makes outbound connections to the overlay and waits for traffic to arrive through them.

That flips the usual attack model. A scanner on the data-hall network finds nothing to connect to. There is no firewall rule to get wrong, because there is no listener.

Legacy apps get this property through a hosting tunneler. The app still listens on 127.0.0.1:5432, as it always did. The tunneler beside it binds the overlay service and forwards accepted connections to that local port. On the client side, a tunneler intercepts a name like pg.team-obs.ziti and carries the connection through the overlay.

Common mistake · “The overlay knows which app called”

Be precise about what this proves. The overlay authenticates the tunneler’s identity, not the process behind it. If two apps share a VM and a tunneler, they share an identity. The finer your identity needs, the more tunnelers or SDKs you need.

Cross-substrate identity

Overlays give you a transport with identity. But many workloads already get identity elsewhere: from SPIRE, from the mesh, from Entra. Federation connects these without merging them.

SPIRE across substrates

SPIRE handles substrate diversity through node attestors, as chapter 6 showed. Each substrate has its own way to prove a node is genuine:

  • OpenShift and Kubernetes nodes: k8s_psat, using projected service account tokens.
  • Physical servers with a TPM: tpm_devid, using a device identity key in the TPM.
  • VMs with an existing certificate: x509pop, proof of possession of a provisioned key.
  • Azure VMs: azure_msi or azure_imds.
  • Bootstrap and labs: join_token, a one-time secret.

After node attestation, the SPIRE agent attests workloads with local facts, such as Unix user or Kubernetes namespace and service account. The output is the same kind of SPIFFE ID on every substrate. That uniformity is the main point.

Federating trust domains

Sometimes you want separate trust domains, for example one per environment or per organisation. SPIFFE federation lets trust domain A fetch the trust bundle of trust domain B from a bundle endpoint. A workload in A can then validate SVIDs from B.

Federation shares the ability to verify, not the right to access. Policy must still name the foreign SPIFFE IDs that are allowed in.

Into Entra and Azure

Microsoft Entra supports workload identity federation. You register a federated identity credential on an app registration or managed identity, naming an issuer, a subject and an audience. A workload presents a SPIFFE JWT-SVID, with the SPIFFE ID as subject, and exchanges it for an Entra access token. No client secret is stored anywhere.

The SPIRE server must publish its signing keys through an OIDC discovery provider that Entra can reach. That endpoint becomes a dependency of every Azure call, which is worth noting in your failure-mode list. Entra also accepts only RS256-signed tokens, while SPIRE signs JWT-SVIDs with EC keys by default, so the SPIRE JWT key type must be changed.

Multi-cluster mesh

A service mesh can also span clusters. The essential requirement is a shared root of trust: all clusters’ mesh CAs chain to one root, or the meshes trust each other’s bundles. Then a workload in cluster A can verify a peer in cluster B.

When clusters sit on separate networks, traffic between them passes through an east-west gateway. In Istio ambient mode it is carried as HBONE, the mTLS tunnel from chapter 9.

Maturity matters here. Istio’s own blog of February 2026 describes ambient multi-network multicluster as Beta and “not ready for production use”; single-network multicluster is still alpha. In OpenShift Service Mesh 3.3, multicluster ambient is a technology preview. Treat cross-cluster ambient as something to prototype, not to build Granvik’s foundation on yet.

A multi-cluster mesh also only covers clusters. VMs can join a mesh in some configurations, but it is the least smooth part of every mesh product.

Export and import across owners

The last piece is not technical plumbing but a contract shape. How does one owner offer a service to another without exposing everything?

PrivateLink as precedent

AWS PrivateLink is the clearest pattern. A provider publishes an endpoint service. A consumer creates an interface endpoint in its own network, which gets a local address there. The provider may require approval of each consumer.

Three properties matter for bubbles. Traffic can only start on the consumer side, so it is one-directional. The consumer reaches one service, not the provider’s network. And the two networks may use overlapping address ranges, because nothing is routed between them.

Consul admin partitions

HashiCorp Consul offers a second precedent. Admin partitions are administrative boundaries for tenants sharing one Consul deployment; namespaces sit inside partitions. Services are invisible across partitions by default.

A provider makes a service visible by writing an exported-services configuration entry in its own partition, naming the consumers. Traffic still needs intentions, Consul’s allow rules. Intentions are written for the destination service, so the provider also names the consumer’s partition as an allowed source. The consumer typically declares the service as an upstream. Multiple partitions require Consul Enterprise.

Both precedents separate two decisions: the provider says “I offer this to you,” and the consumer, or its policy, says “I use it.” Neither grants anything to a third party.

In the bubble model

This chapter supplies most of the machinery for bubbles that span substrates.

Bindings are service policies. An OpenZiti service maps to an exported bubble service. A Bind policy names the provider bubble’s hosting identities; a Dial policy names the consumer bubble’s identities. Role attributes like #bubble-team-obs let you grant trust at the team level while identities stay per atom.

The bubble gateway is a tunneler or a router. For legacy TCP and plain HTTP inside a bubble, a hosting tunneler at the bubble edge gives you a dark service with mTLS between bubbles. The honest cost: inside the bubble, traffic behind the tunneler is not authenticated per atom. The bubble interior becomes a small perimeter again.

SPIRE attestors give one identity format across substrates. OpenShift pods, VMs with TPMs and Azure workloads can all receive SPIFFE IDs under one trust domain. Entra federation lets a bubble reach Azure resources without stored secrets.

PrivateLink and Consul confirm the export/import shape. Both are point-to-point and non-transitive by construction, which is exactly the property bindings need.

Two problems remain. OpenZiti and SPIRE each run their own PKI, so a workload may hold two identities that must be kept consistent. And cross-cluster ambient mesh is not yet mature enough to be the backbone between data halls.

Synthesis

Synthesis

Beyond one cluster, identity needs three things: one identity format on every substrate, a transport that carries identity instead of trusting addresses, and policy written in names. SPIRE’s attestors provide the first, identity-based overlays such as OpenZiti provide the second, including dark services for legacy apps, and federation connects identity systems without merging them, always sharing the ability to verify and never the right to access. PrivateLink and Consul show the contract that ties it together: the provider exports, the consumer imports, and nobody else gains anything.

Putting It Together: Designing with Bubbles

A naming and declaration layer on top of proven mechanisms

The bubble model is best understood as a naming and declaration layer, not a new security mechanism. Git holds who owns what, what is exported and who may import it, all written in stable names. A compiler turns that into the configuration of proven mechanisms: SPIRE registrations, mesh and overlay policies, gateway rules. Everything secret or address-shaped stays out of Git and is produced at runtime. Designing Granvik’s platform then becomes designing that declaration language, and testing that the compiled result keeps its promises.

  • Decide where names and synthetic IDs belong, and explain the cost of each choice
  • Write bubble, import and grant declarations, and apply the inheritance rules to a concrete case
  • Distinguish grants from guardrails and explain why they must combine differently
  • List what belongs in Git and what never does

Before you start

Every earlier chapter ended with In the bubble model. This chapter collects those notes into one design.

The chapter in brief: three layers; names and synthetic IDs; ownership and trust in one object; trust at the level where it is true; bindings; gateways and legacy traffic; delegated identity; what goes in Git; failure modes and tests; open questions.

Three layers: declared, compiled, runtime

Sort every piece of state into one of three layers.

  • Declared: written by humans, reviewed, versioned in Git. Bubbles, owners, exports, imports, trust grants, defaults.
  • Compiled: generated from the declared layer by a controller. SPIRE registration entries, Istio AuthorizationPolicies, OpenZiti service policies, gateway config.
  • Runtime: produced by running systems and never committed. Private keys, SVIDs, tokens, IP addresses, which node a pod landed on.

This makes the vocabulary of chapters 1 and 8 concrete. Git plus the compiler is the policy administration point (PAP, the XACML term from chapter 8; not NIST’s runtime policy administrator). The mesh, the overlay and the gateways are policy enforcement points. Each keeps its own fast local decision logic.

Names and synthetic IDs

Every bubble and every atom gets two identifiers. The conceptual name is a path that humans read: gv/team-obs/loki. The synthetic ID is an opaque, generated value: b-7k2q9x. A name is stable within its context and changes only on a deliberate rename or reorganisation; an ID never changes.

The key decision is which one goes into the cryptographic identity. If the SPIFFE ID contains the name, logs are readable, but a rename means reissuing identities and rewriting every policy that mentions them. If it contains the ID, renames are free, but every log line needs a lookup.

A reasonable starting point is to put IDs in SPIFFE IDs and names everywhere humans look:

spiffe://granvik.internal/b/b-7k2q9x/a/a-p3m8vd

Humans write policy with names. The compiler resolves names to IDs. Because this SPIFFE ID does not encode nesting, a grant to a parent bubble compiles to an explicit list of the IDs beneath it. Dashboards resolve IDs back to names. The name registry, the table mapping one to the other, then becomes critical infrastructure.

Ownership and trust in one object

A bubble is declared once, with its owners and its defaults. Owners are groups in the identity provider, not individuals, so they survive staff changes. Trust and ownership are one unit: the same owners approve the bubble’s grants, and its atoms’ certificates are issued for the bubble. There is no separate trust owner.

# bubbles/gv/team-obs/loki/bubble.yaml
kind: Bubble
name: gv/team-obs/loki
id: b-7k2q9x              # generated once, then immutable
owners:
  group: idp:grp-team-obs
defaults:
  identityTtl: 1h
  substrate: openshift
atoms:
  - name: distributor
    id: a-p3m8vd
    role: ingest
  - name: querier
    id: a-r5t2nw
    role: query
exports:
  - service: push
    port: 3100
    protocol: http
    atoms: [distributor]
  - service: query
    port: 3100
    protocol: http
    atoms: [querier]

Notice what is absent: no IP, no node, no cluster, no data hall. The bubble says what exists and who answers for it, nothing about where.

Trust at the level where it is true

A trust grant says which callers may reach an exported service. It can name a whole bubble or one atom. The common case is coarse: “anything in gv/team-app may push logs.”

Inheritance needs a precise rule:

  1. Nesting passes down names, owners and defaults.
  2. Nesting never passes down blanket access: being inside gv lets you reach nothing in gv.
  3. A grant declared on a provider bubble covers the exports beneath it, until a lower bubble declares its own grant for that service. There inheritance stops: the lowest declared grant applies alone, and higher grants are not merged in.

So a grant on gv/team-obs for “all services” reaches loki/query while loki says nothing about it, but not loki/push, for which loki declares its own grant below.

This creates a tension. Rule 3 makes grants local and predictable. But an organisation also needs guardrails from the top, such as “nothing outside gv may reach anything inside.” AWS SCPs, from chapter 8, show the shape: guardrails never grant; they only cap. A sound model has two rule types. Grants: lowest level wins. Guardrails: every level applies, and they intersect.

Definition · Grant and guardrail

A grant allows callers to reach an exported service; the lowest declared grant for a service applies alone. A guardrail is a ceiling set from above; it never grants, and guardrails at every level intersect.

Bindings: export, import, nothing else

A binding connects one provider export to one consumer bubble. Both sides declare their half, in their own directory, with their own owners approving.

# bubbles/gv/team-app/orders/imports.yaml
kind: Import
consumer: gv/team-app/orders
from: gv/team-obs/loki
service: push
as: logs                  # local name inside the consumer bubble
callers:
  role: any               # trust at bubble level
---
# bubbles/gv/team-obs/loki/grants.yaml
kind: Grant
service: push
allow:
  - bubble: gv/team-app/orders
  - bubble: gv/team-app    # all of team-app, if true
delegation: none

A binding exists only when the import and a matching grant both exist. The compiler then emits one Dial-style rule for the consumer’s identities and one Bind-style rule for the provider’s atoms, plus a mesh or gateway policy.

Non-transitivity must hold by construction. The binding X to Y produces rules that mention only X’s and Y’s IDs. A test can check this mechanically: add the binding, compile, and assert that compiled output for every other bubble is byte-for-byte unchanged.

Common mistake · “Non-transitive bindings keep data apart”

Non-transitivity covers connections, not data. If Y reads from X and passes results to Z, Z sees X’s data. That is a data-classification problem, not a network one.

Gateways and legacy traffic

Each bubble has a conceptual edge. What implements it depends on substrate:

  • On OpenShift with ambient mode: ztunnel for mTLS and L4 identity, a waypoint for HTTP-level rules.
  • On VMs or physical hosts: an OpenZiti hosting tunneler, or a proxy with a SPIFFE identity.
  • For plain TCP apps: a tunneler at both edges; the app never sees a certificate.

Where the edge is shared by several atoms, per-atom identity stops at the edge. That is an acceptable trade for legacy systems, but write it down per bubble. A field such as edgeMode: per-atom | shared makes the weaker cases visible.

Delegated identity

When a service acts for a user, two identities travel together: the workload’s SVID on the connection and the user’s token in the request. The grant should say which is required.

As in chapter 7, the calling atom performs token exchange (RFC 8693) before the call: it swaps the user’s token for a narrower one whose audience is the provider’s service, with an act claim naming itself. It does this over mTLS, so the token is bound to its certificate (RFC 8705). The provider edge checks the audience, and that the act claim and certificate binding match the connection’s peer. The grant’s delegation field above is where this choice is declared.

What goes in Git, and what never does

In Git: bubble declarations, synthetic IDs once generated, exports, imports, grants, guardrails, defaults, the trust domain name, and per-site overlays that map bubbles to substrates in that site.

Never in Git:

  • Private keys, CA keys, SVIDs, tokens or join tokens.
  • IP addresses, CIDRs, node names, hostnames of specific machines.
  • Compiled output, which is regenerated and diffed, not edited.
  • Attestation evidence and live registration state.

Portability follows from this split. Bringing the same repository up in another data hall means a new site overlay, a new SPIRE server and CA under the same or a federated trust domain, and a fresh compile. If anything else must change, a location detail has leaked into the declared layer.

Example · Rule 4711, rewritten

At Granvik the reporting server that once needed the payroll database would today be an atom in a bubble, say gv/team-finance/reports. The payroll bubble would export db and grant it to gv/team-finance/reports alone, and the reports bubble would import it. When the reporting server is retired, its bubble is deleted, the import disappears, the binding stops existing, and the next compile removes every rule it produced. Nothing is left pointing at an address, because nothing ever did.

Failure modes and the tests that find them

Each property needs a test that can fail, in CI and in game days.

  • Swap test. Replace the overlay with the mesh for one bubble, or the reverse. Only compiled output should change, never declarations. If declarations change, the model is coupled to a product.
  • Leaked certificate. Steal one atom’s SVID. Blast radius should be one atom, for at most one TTL, reaching only services that atom’s bubble imports. Removing its registration entry should stop renewal.
  • Binding isolation. Add or remove any binding; assert zero diff in all third-party bubbles’ compiled policy.
  • Reorganisation. Move loki from team-obs to team-platform. Its ID and SPIFFE ID stay the same. Grants that named gv/team-obs as a whole should now visibly exclude it, and the diff should show exactly that.
  • Site move. Compile for hall B with only the site overlay changed. Compare against hall A.
  • Registry outage. If name resolution is down, enforcement must keep working on cached compiled policy.

Open questions

  1. One trust domain or several? One per environment is simple. One per site needs federation. One per top-level bubble gives harder isolation but more bundles to manage.
  2. Who issues certificates? SPIRE, the mesh CA and OpenZiti’s controller each want to be a CA. Can one root, with intermediates per system, keep them consistent?
  3. How deep can nesting go before it hurts? Three levels are easy to reason about. Seven are not. Should depth be capped?
  4. What exactly is an atom on a VM? A process, a Unix user, a systemd unit? Each needs a different workload attestor and gives different guarantees.
  5. Who approves a grant? Provider owners alone, or also a platform guardrail review for cross-organisation bindings?
  6. How are synthetic IDs minted and kept unique across sites without a shared live database?
  7. What happens to existing connections when a binding is removed? Revocation is immediate in policy, but long-lived TCP sessions may outlive it.
  8. How does Azure fit? As another site with its own SPIRE server, or as a foreign trust domain reached only through Entra federation?
  9. Is the bubble interior trusted? Where edgeMode: shared, an attacker inside the bubble moves freely. Is that acceptable for some bubbles, and who decides?

In the bubble model

In its shortest form: a bubble is a directory in Git with owners, atoms, exports, imports and grants, all in names. A compiler turns names into IDs and declarations into mechanism-specific policy. SPIRE gives every atom a short-lived identity, wherever it runs. Edges enforce bindings with the mesh, the overlay or a tunneler.

It is strong where it reuses proven parts and keeps secrets and addresses out of Git, and weakest at three seams. The name registry is a new piece of critical infrastructure. Several CAs must stay consistent. And shared edges quietly bring a small perimeter back inside each bubble. Design the tests for those seams first.

Synthesis

Synthesis

The bubble model adds no new cryptography. It adds a place to say, in stable names, who owns what, what is offered and who may use it, and a compiler that turns those statements into SPIRE entries, mesh policies and overlay rules. Two kinds of rule keep it predictable: grants, where the lowest declaration wins, and guardrails, which only cap. Secrets and addresses never enter the declared layer, so the same repository can be rebuilt anywhere. Its risks sit at the seams (the name registry, several CAs, shared edges), and the tests in this chapter are aimed at exactly those.

Check yourself

Test yourself

gv/team-obs grants push to all of gv/team-app. loki declares its own grant for push allowing only gv/team-app/orders. Can gv/team-app/billing push to loki? Why?

Show answer

No. loki declared its own grant for push, so inheritance stops there and only orders is allowed; the higher grant to all of team-app is not merged in.

Test yourself

The SPIFFE ID specification says something about the meaning of the path, and section 4.1.1 gives advice on volatile information. What do they imply for putting bubble names in SPIFFE IDs?

Show answer

Path meaning is left to the implementer, so opaque IDs are allowed. The advice to leave out volatile assertions supports using synthetic IDs, not reorganisable names, in SPIFFE IDs.

Test yourself

Using RFC 8705’s rule on which certificate a bound token must be presented with, explain why the calling atom, not the provider’s edge, should perform the token exchange.

Show answer

RFC 8705 requires the protected resource request to use the same certificate that was used at the token endpoint. If the provider edge exchanged the token, it would be bound to the edge’s certificate, not the calling atom’s, and would prove nothing about the caller.

Test yourself

Which test proves a binding is non-transitive, and what kind of leak does non-transitivity not prevent?

Show answer

The binding isolation test: add or remove a binding, recompile, and assert zero diff in every third bubble’s compiled policy. It does not stop data flowing onward: if Y reads from X and passes results to Z, Z sees X’s data.

The primary sources, in the order that builds vocabulary before detail

Read the primary sources in an order that builds vocabulary before detail: principles first, then workload identity, then people and delegation, then policy, then the products you might deploy. For each source, the goal is to answer one question for your own design, not to finish it. Skim generously; most of these documents are reference material, and their value is in a few sections.

How to read them

Read 1 to 5 in order. Then read 6 to 9 when you design delegation, 10 to 12 when you design grants and guardrails, and 13 to 15 when you choose enforcement points.

For each source, write down one sentence: what it changes in your design. If a source changes nothing, you skimmed the right amount.

The US standards body’s definition of zero trust, published in 2020. Read it first because every vendor quotes it. Focus on sections 2 and 3: the tenets, and the logical components (policy engine, policy administrator, policy enforcement point). Map each component to a part of your design as you read. Why this one · 2. Google’s BeyondCorp papers

Six short articles, originally in USENIX ;login:, describing how Google removed its privileged network. Read closely “A New Approach to Enterprise Security” and “The Access Proxy”. Note that BeyondCorp is about users reaching services; workload-to-workload is a different problem. https://research.google/pubs/beyondcorp-a-new-approach-to-enterprise-security/ (all six are listed at https://beyondcorp.com/)

By Razi Rais, Christina Morillo, Evan Gilman and Doug Barth. It connects the principles to practice better than any single paper.
