      Reader and scope

      The person accountable for keeping an organisation's important services running through severe disruption: a head of operations, a system or service owner, a preparedness or continuity coordinator, an IT manager, an architect in that role.

      Knows their own organisation and roughly what outsourcing and cloud mean. Doesn't know resilience engineering. Doesn't need to code.

      In: deciding what must keep working, finding what it really stands on, building fallbacks before the day, leading, communicating and staffing through weeks of disruption, exercising and learning. All-hazards; IT-heavy but also power, telecom, suppliers and people; Swedish preparedness and total defence as the frame, international cases.

      Out: how to build the technical solutions (one engineering appendix), detailed security controls and threat intelligence, ISO 22301 certification, legal advice (a dated regulatory appendix).

      Follows from one reader: no decision-maker box (the reader is the decision-maker); engineering pointers go to one appendix (today's A–D merged); technical terms in the cases (control plane, DNS, domain controller) explained in plain words where used.

      Big ideas

      • Disruption will come. The questions are when, for how long and what keeps working, so plan by effect and duration, not by cause.
      • The outcome is decided by the shape of your dependencies and whether they fail together. It shows most clearly in contrasts inside one incident: Umeå and Primula, Coop's co-ops, the British Library's payroll.
      • What you need in the crisis must exist, and have been used, before it. Lead times are months.
      • Recovery is a programme of weeks to months, and your provider's recovery is not yours.
      • Survivability is lost one reasonable decision at a time, and kept by practice.
